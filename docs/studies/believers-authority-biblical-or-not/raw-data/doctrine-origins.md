- The spiritual is the cause of all physical effects
- The human mind, through positive mental attitude and positive confession, has the power to create its own reality

- Words and thoughts have creative power over material circumstances

#### Kenyon's Time at Emerson College

Kenyon attended Emerson College of Oratory in Boston for one year in 1892. At the time, the college's founder Charles Wesley Emerson was a Unitarian minister. The school was described by scholars as "a hub for New Thought philosophy" and was "immersed in the metaphysical cults, and the underlying New Thought" (McConnell, *A Different Gospel*).

**The scholarly debate:** Kenyon biographer Joe McIntyre argues that Kenyon developed his positive confession teaching primarily "from the teachings of Holiness Movement, Faith Cure and Higher Life movement ministers of the late 19th Century," not from New Thought influence. However, D.R. McConnell and others demonstrated that Kenyon was "widely read" concerning the metaphysical cults. Ern Baxter recalled Kenyon speaking positively of Mary Baker Eddy's *Key to the Scriptures*. John Kennington recalled a conversation in which Kenyon admitted he had "freely used ideas from Mary Baker-Eddy."

#### Kenyon's Key Theological Innovations

**1. "Revelation Knowledge" vs. "Sense Knowledge"**

Kenyon divided all human knowledge into two categories:

- **Sense Knowledge**: "All the knowledge that is taught in our schools, colleges and universities has been gained through our five senses." Sense Knowledge "cannot find God, can never know Him." It gives "theories" but not reality.
- **Revelation Knowledge**: Knowledge that "we receive only from the Word of God." Revelation knowledge gives "reality."

**Where this came from:** This mirrors the New Thought/Christian Science distinction between material (illusory) reality perceived by the senses and spiritual (true) reality perceived by the mind/spirit. Mary Baker Eddy taught that physical senses deceive and that spiritual perception reveals truth. Kenyon Christianized this by replacing "spiritual perception" with "revelation from God's Word" — but the underlying dualistic structure (senses lie, spiritual knowledge is true) comes directly from metaphysical philosophy, not from Scripture.

**2. "Power of Attorney" in the Name of Jesus**

From *The Wonderful Name of Jesus* (1927): Kenyon recounted a conversation with a lawyer during a sermon in Tennessee, where the lawyer affirmed that "if language means anything, then Jesus gave to the church the Power of Attorney." Kenyon developed this into a systematic teaching that:

- Prayer is placed on a "purely legal basis"
- Jesus has given believers "the legal right to use His Name"
- Believers have "the same authority He had when He was here"
- The believer's "position in Christ" gives them "the same standing with the Father that Christ had"

**Kenyon's quote:** "With that Word, we conquer disease...Cancer, in the Name of Jesus, shrivel up and stop being."

**3. Positive Confession**

Kenyon taught that spoken words in faith carry divine power:

- "Faith talks in the language of God. Doubt talks in the language of man."
- "When you confess your weakness and your disease you are openly confessing that the Word of God is not true."
- "What I confess, I possess."

**Where this came from:** This directly mirrors New Thought's "affirmation" technique — speaking positive statements to create positive reality. The language shifted from "affirmation" to "confession," and the power source shifted from "universal mind" to "God's Word," but the mechanism is identical: spoken words create physical reality.

**4. "Identification" Theology**

Kenyon taught that believers are identified with Christ in such a way that they share His authority, power, and standing. "The New Creation is just like the Master. He is its head. He is the Vine, you are the branch. As He is, so are you."

**5. The Spiritual Death of Jesus**

From *What Happened from the Cross to the Throne* (1945): Kenyon was the first within the church to teach that Jesus died spiritually (not just physically), took on Satan's nature, was dragged to hell, suffered there for three days, and was "born again" — becoming "the first born again man."

Kenyon wrote: "Jesus died twice on the cross. I knew this for many years before I found the actual proof in Scripture." He claimed to find proof in Isaiah 53:9, arguing that "the word 'death' is plural in the Hebrew."

**6. Believers as "Little Gods"**

Kenyon taught that man is "a little god 'in God's class'" who can use "the same faith-force" that God uses.

#### What Kenyon Was NOT

Critically, Kenyon was **not** Pentecostal or Charismatic. He actually criticized the Pentecostal movement as "as destructive as it was instructive" and rejected speaking in tongues as overly subjective. He was a Baptist pastor and Bible school founder. His theology was a hybrid: evangelical language wrapped around metaphysical concepts.

As McConnell concluded, Kenyon created a "Christianized metaphysical cult" — a response to mainline churches' perceived anti-supernatural tendencies, but one that drew its power concepts from New Thought rather than from Scripture.

#### What MacMillan Actually Taught

MacMillan's teaching was rooted in Ephesians 1–2 and emphasized:
- Believers possess **delegated** authority from Christ based on their **position** in Him
- Authority operates through "throne-union" with Christ via prayer and faith
- The "policeman analogy": a police officer's badge gives authority to stop traffic because "the whole power of the state is behind him" — the authority is delegated, not inherent
- Believers can bind demonic forces and claim protection through "binding and loosing"

**Critical difference from Kenyon/Hagin:** MacMillan grounded authority in positional truth (Ephesians) and covenant relationship with God, **emphasizing consecration and obedience as prerequisites**. His authority operated **within God's sovereign will** through intercession. He focused on missionary spiritual warfare, not personal prosperity or material gain.

MacMillan "would not approve of all that is currently taught...such as 'little gods' and 'name it and claim it'" (Paul L. King, scholar of MacMillan's work).

Merrill Unger (Dallas Theological Seminary) and Wayne Grudem included MacMillan in theological bibliographies as a credible source on spiritual warfare — evidence that evangelical scholars viewed his work as orthodox.

#### Why This Matters

MacMillan represents an **orthodox stream** of teaching about spiritual authority that existed independently of Kenyon's metaphysical-influenced version. Hagin plagiarized **both** — taking from MacMillan's orthodox framework and from Kenyon's heterodox innovations, then blending them into a single system that appeared biblical on the surface but carried Kenyon's metaphysical DNA underneath.

#### The Theological System

The Word of Faith movement developed a complete theological system from Kenyon's innovations:

1. **Faith as a Force**: Faith is not trust in God but a substance/force that believers wield. God Himself used faith to create the universe. Believers can use the same force.

2. **Words as Containers**: Spoken words are "spiritual containers" that carry faith-force into the material world. Positive words create positive reality; negative words create negative reality.

7. **Prosperity as Divine Right**: Material wealth is always God's will for believers. Poverty indicates lack of faith. Giving to ministries triggers supernatural financial returns.

## The Theological Innovations (With Sources)

### Innovation 1: "Spiritual Death of Jesus" — Did Jesus Die Spiritually and Go to Hell?

**Originated by**: E.W. Kenyon, *What Happened from the Cross to the Throne* (1945)

**The teaching**: Jesus died spiritually on the cross, was separated from God, took on Satan's sinful nature, was dragged to hell by Satan, suffered demonic torment for three days, and was then "born again" by God — becoming "the first born again man."

**Academic assessment**: William P. Atkinson's PhD thesis at the University of Edinburgh (*A Theological Appraisal of the Doctrine that Jesus Died Spiritually, as Taught by Kenyon, Hagin and Copeland*, 2007; winner of the Award of Excellence of the Foundation for Pentecostal Scholarship, 2010) found that the alleged "spiritual death" incorporates three major elements: (1) Jesus was separated from God, (2) partook of a sinful, satanic nature, and (3) was Satan's prey. Atkinson traced the doctrine's origin to Kenyon and its transmission through Hagin to Copeland.

**Why it has no biblical precedent**: 
- "It is finished" (John 19:30) indicates the atonement was completed on the cross
- Christ remained sinless throughout (1 Peter 2:22; Hebrews 4:15)
- Christ's deity is inseparable from His person — He could not "become sin" in an ontological sense
- Old Testament sacrificial animals remained holy throughout the sacrificial ceremony
- No church father, Reformer, or pre-Kenyon theologian ever taught this

### Innovation 2: "Born Again Jesus" — Did Jesus Need to Be Born Again?

**Originated by**: E.W. Kenyon (derived from his "spiritual death" doctrine)

**The teaching**: After three days of torment in hell, God spoke and Jesus was "born again" — His spirit was recreated. He was the "firstborn from the dead" (Colossians 1:18) in the sense of being the first person to be spiritually regenerated.

**Why it has no biblical precedent**:
- "Firstborn from the dead" refers to resurrection priority, not spiritual regeneration
- Being "born again" implies prior spiritual death/sinfulness — attributing sin to the sinless Son of God
- No historical Christian tradition ever interpreted Colossians 1:18 as spiritual rebirth

### Innovation 3: "Little Gods" Doctrine — Are Believers Gods?

**Originated by**: E.W. Kenyon (from New Thought concept of human divinity)

**Key quotes**:
- Copeland: "You don't have a god in you, you are one."
- Copeland: "Adam was created, not subordinate to God, but as a god."
- Hagin Jr.: "You are as much the incarnation of God as Jesus Christ was."

**Why it has no biblical precedent**:
- Isaiah 43:10: "Before me there was no God formed, neither shall there be after me"
- The passage most often cited (Psalm 82:6, "I have said, Ye are gods") is addressing human judges who will "die like men" (v. 7) — a rebuke, not a promotion
- Historic Christianity unanimously taught the Creator/creature distinction

### Innovation 4: "Faith as a Force" — Is Faith a Substance Believers Wield?

**Originated by**: E.W. Kenyon (from New Thought's "mind power" concept)

**The teaching**: Faith is not trust in God's character but a metaphysical force/substance that even God uses. God created the universe by "releasing faith." Believers can use this same force to create material reality.

**New Thought parallel**: New Thought taught that the human mind could manipulate spiritual forces to reshape material reality. Kenyon replaced "mind" with "faith" and "universal principle" with "God" — but the mechanism remained identical: invisible force wielded by human will produces material effects.

**Why it has no biblical precedent**:
- Hebrews 11:1 ("faith is the substance of things hoped for") describes faith as confident trust, not as a metaphysical force
- Biblical faith is always directed **toward God** — it is trust in a Person, not a force to be wielded
- No biblical text describes God as using faith; God speaks and it happens by His sovereign power

## Scholarly Critiques (With Citations)

### D.R. McConnell — *A Different Gospel* (1988; updated edition 1995)

McConnell's work, originally his master's thesis at Oral Roberts University, established three foundational claims:

1. **The faith movement represents "metaphysical thought masquerading in evangelical garb."** He demonstrated that Kenyon's key theological concepts — positive confession, sense knowledge vs. revelation knowledge, the spiritual death of Jesus, believers as gods — parallel New Thought/Christian Science teachings and were developed during or after Kenyon's time at Emerson College.

2. **Kenneth Hagin plagiarized E.W. Kenyon "both repeatedly and extensively."** Side-by-side comparison across eight books showed "word-for-word uniformity...beyond the bounds of coincidence."

### Hank Hanegraaff — *Christianity in Crisis* (1993; updated *Christianity in Crisis: 21st Century*, 2009)

Hanegraaff, president of the Christian Research Institute, built on McConnell's research to identify five critical **F.L.A.W.S.** in Word of Faith teaching:

- **F** = Faith in faith (faith as a force rather than trust in God)
- **L** = Little gods (deification of believers)
- **A** = Atonement atrocities (spiritual death of Jesus, born-again Jesus)
- **W** = Wealth and want (prosperity as divine right)

The original 1993 edition focused on Kenyon, Hagin, Copeland, Hinn, and Hagee. The 2009 update incorporated newer teachers including Osteen, Meyer, Dollar, Jakes, and Parsley.

### Kate Bowler — *Blessed: A History of the American Prosperity Gospel* (Oxford University Press, 2013)

Bowler, assistant professor of religion at Duke Divinity School (adapted from her PhD dissertation), spent a decade traveling the United States and Canada visiting megachurches, archives, television studios, and revivals. She visited 25% of all prosperity megachurches and participated for 18 months in a small African American prosperity church.

Bowler identified four central themes of the prosperity gospel: **faith, wealth, health, and victory**. She traced the movement's origins from late 19th century roots through its flourishing during WWII-era Pentecostal revivals to its maturation after the 1960s.

This is the first academic book to fully explore the origins, unifying themes, and major figures of the movement from a historical perspective.

### Robert Jackson — Themelios Article: "Prosperity Theology and the Faith Movement"

## Was This Doctrine EVER Taught Before Kenyon?

### What the Church Fathers Taught About Spiritual Authority

The early church taught that believers have authority over demons — but in a completely different framework from the Word of Faith doctrine:

- **Justin Martyr** (c. 100–165): Testified that "even the demons do fear; and at this day, when they are exorcised in the name of Jesus Christ, crucified under Pontius Pilate, they are overcome." But this was exorcism through Christ's name and power, not an autonomous "believer's authority."

- **Tertullian** (c. 155–220): Affirmed that Christians could cast out demons, but always by the power of Christ, not by any inherent authority of the believer.

- **The early church fathers**: Believed "freedom from and authority over the devil are marks of a true Christian," but this was understood as Christ's authority exercised through believers, not believers wielding independent spiritual force.

**Critical distinction**: Patristic sources emphasized submission to God and to ecclesiastical authority. The authority over demons was **derived** and **delegated**, exercised through prayer, fasting, and invocation of Christ's name — never through "positive confession," "faith force," or claiming one's rights as a "little god."

### What the Reformers Taught

- **Martin Luther**: Maintained belief in exorcism and demonic deliverance. His Large Catechism included making the sign of the cross to drive away the devil. Lutheran liturgies included exorcism in conjunction with baptism. But Luther's framework was God's sovereign power working through ordinary means, not believers commanding reality.

- **John Calvin**: Taught that demons had been essentially banished from the world after the resurrection. He "did away with exorcism and deliverance, at least for Christians." His emphasis on God's sovereignty left no room for autonomous "believer's authority."

### Was the Specific "Believer's Authority" Doctrine Ever Taught Before Kenyon?

**No.** Here is what did NOT exist before Kenyon:

1. No church father taught that believers are "little gods" or share God's nature ontologically
2. No Reformer taught that faith is a "force" or "substance" that believers wield
3. No pre-1890 Protestant taught that spoken words create physical reality
4. No historical Christian tradition taught the "spiritual death of Jesus"
5. No theologian before Kenyon taught that Jesus was "born again" in hell
6. No orthodox tradition taught that prayer operates on a "legal basis" where God is obligated to respond
7. No pre-New Thought teaching framed "revelation knowledge" vs. "sense knowledge" as competing epistemological systems

### What DID Exist Before Kenyon

**The Keswick/Higher Life tradition** (1870s–1920s) taught concepts of spiritual authority that were **orthodox**:

- Andrew Murray (1885): Believers are "kings and priests" through whom God rules — but this was through prayer and submission, not autonomous command
- A.T. Pierson (1895): "Authority of faith" based on Luke 10:19 — but within God's sovereign will
- Jessie Penn-Lewis (1897): Believer's position in Christ (Ephesians 1–2) — but focused on spiritual warfare, not material prosperity
- A.B. Simpson (CMA founder): Believers receive *exousia* (authority) over spiritual enemies — but Simpson distinguished this from *dunamis* (power), emphasizing that authority is delegated, not inherent
- **John MacMillan (1932)**: The definitive systematic treatment of "the authority of the believer" — but firmly orthodox, focused on missionary spiritual warfare, requiring consecration and obedience, operating within God's will

**Russell Kelso Carter** (1884): Published *The Atonement for Sin and Sickness*, demonstrating that elements of faith-healing theology existed before Kenyon. But Carter's teaching was about trusting God for healing, not about wielding faith as a force.

### The Core Problem

The "believer's authority" doctrine as commonly taught today is **not** a recovery of biblical truth but a **19th-century metaphysical philosophy** that was:

1. **Developed** by Phineas Quimby and the New Thought movement (1840s–1890s)
2. **Christianized** by E.W. Kenyon after exposure at Emerson College (1892 onward)
3. **Plagiarized and systematized** by Kenneth Hagin (1950s–1970s)
4. **Spread globally** through RHEMA and televangelist networks (1974–present)
5. **Absorbed unconsciously** by mainstream churches that adopted Word of Faith vocabulary without knowing its origins

### What the Bible Actually Teaches About Authority

The biblical concept of spiritual authority is fundamentally different from the Word of Faith version:

- **Authority is delegated, not inherent**: Jesus told the seventy-two, "I have given you authority" (Luke 10:19) — it was His to give, not theirs to claim
- **Authority operates within God's sovereign will**: Jesus prayed, "Not my will, but yours be done" (Luke 22:42) — the model prayer is submission, not command
- **Faith is trust in a Person, not a force to wield**: "Abraham believed God, and it was counted to him for righteousness" (Romans 4:3)
- **Suffering is normative, not a failure of faith**: Paul's thorn in the flesh (2 Corinthians 12:7–10); "all that will live godly in Christ Jesus shall suffer persecution" (2 Timothy 3:12)
- **Authority over demons is Christ's power working through believers**: "In my name shall they cast out devils" (Mark 16:17) — "in my name," not "by their own authority"
