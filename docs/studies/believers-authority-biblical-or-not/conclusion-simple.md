# Does the Bible Teach "Believer's Authority"?

---

## The Question

There's a popular teaching in some Christian circles that believers possess spiritual authority — that we can exercise power over demons, circumstances, and spiritual forces through our own faith-empowered authority. But is this what the Bible actually teaches? Or is every instance of spiritual authority and power something that comes FROM Jesus Christ THROUGH the Holy Spirit, depending entirely on faith, obedience, prayer, and abiding — nothing in yourself?

This study examined 52 passages across both Testaments, parsed the Greek words for "power" and "authority," traced every commissioning text, and tested the concept against the full weight of biblical evidence.

The answer is clear: **believers never possess inherent spiritual authority. Every instance of authority is delegated by Christ, exercised "in the name of Jesus," powered by the Holy Spirit, and conditioned on ongoing dependence.**

---

## The Lexical Key: Two Different Greek Words

The entire study turns on a distinction most English Bibles obscure. In Luke 10:19, Jesus says: "Behold, I give unto you **power** to tread on serpents and scorpions, and over all the **power** of the enemy."

The English word "power" appears twice. But in Greek, two completely different words are used:

- The first "power" is **exousia** (G1849) — meaning delegated authority, permission, the *right* to act. It comes from *exesti*, "it is permitted."
- The second "power" is **dynamis** (G1411) — meaning inherent force, ability, raw capability.

Jesus gives the disciples **exousia** (permission to act) over the enemy's **dynamis** (inherent power). The believers receive delegated permission. The inherent power belongs to the enemy — and the power that defeats it belongs to God. Believers are never said to possess dynamis as their own.

Only in Jesus do exousia and dynamis converge. No other being in Scripture possesses both rightful authority AND inherent supernatural power simultaneously.

---

## Authority Over Demons: The Strongest Test Case

If believers had inherent authority anywhere, it would be in casting out demons. But every commissioning text tells the same story.

**Matthew 10:1** — "He gave them power (exousia) over unclean spirits." The verb is *edoken* (aorist of didomi, "to give") — a specific moment of bestowal. Before that moment, the disciples didn't have it.

**Luke 9:1** — Luke is the only Gospel writer who uses BOTH dynamis and exousia in the commissioning. Jesus gave both because the disciples possessed neither.

**Luke 10:17** — When the seventy return celebrating, they say: "Lord, even the devils are subject unto us **through thy name**." They identify the source themselves — not "through our authority" but "through thy name."

**The Failed Exorcism (Mark 9:28-29)** — The disciples had received authority, used it successfully, and then failed. Why? Jesus said: "because of your unbelief" and "this kind goeth not out but by prayer." The authority wasn't revoked — they stopped depending on God. Delegated authority requires ongoing dependence.

**The Sons of Sceva (Acts 19:13-16)** — This is the definitive negative example. Seven exorcists tried to use the name of Jesus as a formula: "We adjure you by Jesus whom Paul preacheth." The demon's response is devastating:

"Jesus I know (*ginosko* — personal, experiential recognition), and Paul I know (*epistamai* — factual awareness of delegated ministry); but who are ye?"

Three tiers of authority recognition: Jesus (inherent), Paul (delegated), Sceva's sons (none). The demon then physically overpowered them. The name of Jesus without genuine relationship is not merely ineffective — it is dangerous.

Even Jesus attributed His exorcism power to the Spirit: "If I cast out devils **by the Spirit of God**, then the kingdom of God is come unto you" (Matthew 12:28). If Christ operated in dependence on the Spirit, any claim of independent believer authority exceeds what Jesus Himself claimed.

---

## Peter's Explicit Denial

After healing the lame man at the Beautiful Gate, Peter immediately and publicly denies personal power:

"Why look ye so earnestly on us, as though by **our own power** (*idia dynamis*) or **holiness** (*eusebeia*) we had made this man to walk?" (Acts 3:12)

Peter denies BOTH inherent ability (dynamis) AND earned worthiness (eusebeia). The intensifier *idia* ("own") makes the denial emphatic — specifically THEIR OWN power.

Then he identifies the actual source: "His name through faith in his name hath made this man strong" (Acts 3:16). The grammatical subject doing the strengthening is "his name," not the apostles.

And then Peter goes even further: "the faith which is **by him**" — even the faith that enabled the miracle is attributed to Christ, not to Peter.

This is the single most explicit apostolic denial of inherent believer authority in the New Testament.

---

## "Without Me Ye Can Do Nothing"

John 15:5 rules out inherent authority at the most fundamental level:

"I am the vine, ye are the branches: He that abideth in me, and I in him, the same bringeth forth much fruit: for without me ye can do nothing."

The Greek is emphatic. *Choris emou* ("apart from me") plus *ou dynasthe poiein ouden* — a double negative intensifier meaning "you are absolutely not able to do anything." The present tense *dynasthe* indicates ongoing inability, not a momentary lapse.

The vine-branch metaphor is biological: a severed branch has no life. But the metaphor also preserves real agency — "the same bringeth forth much fruit." The fruitfulness is real. The branch genuinely bears fruit. But the life producing the fruit comes entirely from the vine.

---

## Strength Made Perfect in Weakness

Paul's theology in 2 Corinthians 12:9 directly and explicitly contradicts any concept of inherent believer authority:

"My strength is made perfect in weakness."

The verb *teleitai* (present passive) means "is being brought to completion continuously." Divine power reaches its fullest expression through human weakness. The relationship is inversely proportional — more weakness enables more divine power.

Paul doesn't ask God to make him stronger. He embraces weakness: "Most gladly therefore will I rather glory in my infirmities, that the power of Christ may rest upon me."

The power "rests upon" (*episkenose* — "tabernacles upon") Paul. It is ON him from outside, not IN him as a possession.

And 2 Corinthians 4:7 states God's design purpose: "We have this treasure in earthen vessels, **that the excellency of the power may be of God, and not of us**." God deliberately uses weak vessels so the power is attributed to Him, not to the vessel.

---

## Three Power Words, All God's

Ephesians 6:10 stacks three Greek power words and attributes all three to God:

"Be strong (*endunamousthe*, G1743 — present passive imperative: 'keep being strengthened by another') in the Lord, and in the power (*kratei*, G2904 — sovereign dominion power) of his might (*ischyos*, G2479 — inherent strength)."

The passive construction means the believer is the recipient of strengthening, not its source. *Kratos* and *ischus* belong to God. The same vocabulary appears in Ephesians 1:19-20 describing the power God "wrought in Christ, when he raised him from the dead."

The armor of God is GOD's armor, worn by the believer. The weapons are divine; the believer wears them but did not forge them.

---

## The OT Foundation

This is not a New Testament innovation. Zechariah 4:6 establishes the principle:

"Not by might (*chayil*, H2428 — organizational/military strength), nor by power (*koach*, H3581 — personal force/capacity), but by my spirit, saith the LORD of hosts."

Both categories of human power — organizational and personal — are explicitly negated. Only God's Spirit accomplishes the work.

Isaiah 40:29-31 promises: "He giveth power to the faint; and to them that have no might he increaseth strength." The recipients are described by their weakness, not their authority.

Second Chronicles 16:9: "The eyes of the LORD run to and fro throughout the whole earth, to shew himself strong in the behalf of them whose heart is perfect toward him." God actively searches for dependence so He can display HIS power through them.

---

## The "Yet Not I" Pattern

Paul establishes a pattern that preserves both genuine agency and divine sourcing:

- **Galatians 2:20** — "I live; yet not I, but Christ liveth in me."
- **1 Corinthians 15:10** — "I laboured more abundantly than they all: yet not I, but the grace of God which was with me."
- **Philippians 2:12-13** — "Work out your own salvation with fear and trembling. For it is God which worketh in you both to will and to do."

The agency is real. Paul genuinely labors. But the source is entirely divine. God works both the willing (even the desire to obey) and the doing (the capacity to execute).

Philippians 4:13 is often misread as personal empowerment: "I can do all things through Christ which strengtheneth me." The Greek shows the opposite — *endunamounti* (present active participle) means Christ "continuously empowers." Paul is strong only while Christ is actively pouring strength into him.

---

## Faith, Prayer, and Fasting: Postures of Dependence

The conditions for exercising spiritual power are all forms of dependence, not techniques for accumulating personal authority:

- **Faith** is God's gift (Ephesians 2:8), authored by Jesus (Hebrews 12:2), produced by the Spirit (Galatians 5:22)
- **Prayer** is appeal to God's power — by definition, an acknowledgment that you don't have it
- **Fasting** is self-denial — emptying, not accumulating
- **Abiding** is continuous connection to the source (John 15:4-7)

When Jesus said "this kind goeth not out but by prayer" (Mark 9:29), He identified prayer as the mechanism of ongoing dependence — not a technique for charging up personal authority.

---

## Where Did "Believer's Authority" Come From?

Multiple scholars trace the modern "believer's authority" concept to E.W. Kenyon (1867-1948), who fused Christianity with New Thought metaphysical philosophy during his time at Emerson College of Oratory in Boston. Kenneth Hagin later plagiarized and popularized Kenyon's teachings.

A peer-reviewed article in *Themelios* (The Gospel Coalition) identifies three exegetical errors: extracting promises to national Israel and applying them to individuals, literalizing figurative language, and distorting prayer into "positive confession" formulas.

The doctrine was not taught in historic Christianity before Kenyon. It entered the church from metaphysical philosophy, not from biblical exegesis.

But the historical analysis is secondary. The biblical text itself — 52 passages across both Testaments — is the primary evidence. The doctrine fails on exegetical grounds before the historical evidence is even considered.

---

## The Bottom Line

The believer's position is that of:

- A **branch** connected to the vine — genuinely bearing fruit, but all life comes from the vine
- An **earthen vessel** carrying divine treasure — the excellency of the power is God's, not ours
- A **soldier** wearing God's armor — the weapons are divine, the soldier didn't forge them
- An **ambassador** acting in Christ's name — representing His authority, not claiming our own

Never that of an independent authority wielding personal power.

The power is always God's. The glory is always God's. The authority is always Christ's. And the condition for it flowing through the believer is always the same: faith, obedience, prayer, fasting, abiding — the postures of dependence, not the postures of possession.

## Appendix: Where Did "Believer's Authority" Come From?

The modern "believer's authority" doctrine did not come from Scripture. Its history can be traced with scholarly precision.

### The Transmission Chain

**New Thought metaphysics (1830s-1890s)** → Phineas Quimby's "mental science" → Mary Baker Eddy's Christian Science → Ralph Waldo Trine's *In Tune with the Infinite* → **the intellectual environment at Emerson College of Oratory in Boston**

↓

**E.W. Kenyon (1867-1948)** — Baptist pastor who attended Emerson College in 1892 for one year, immersed in the New Thought environment. He admitted to having "freely used ideas from Mary Baker-Eddy" (per colleague John Kennington). Key innovations with NO biblical precedent:

- "Revelation knowledge vs. sense knowledge" — mirrors Christian Science's spiritual/material dualism
- "Power of attorney" in the name of Jesus — prayer as a "legal basis" where believers have "the same standing with the Father that Christ had"
- "What I confess, I possess" — New Thought's "affirmation" technique relabeled as "confession"
- "Spiritual death of Jesus" — Jesus died spiritually, took on Satan's nature, went to hell, was "born again"
- Believers as "little gods" in "God's class"

↓

**Kenneth Hagin (1917-2003)** — Plagiarized Kenyon "both repeatedly and extensively" (D.R. McConnell, *A Different Gospel*, 1988). Ruth Kenyon (Kenyon's daughter) confirmed: "pure plagiarism." Hagin ALSO plagiarized John MacMillan's orthodox 1932 "Authority of the Believer" — blending MacMillan's biblical vocabulary with Kenyon's metaphysical content. Founded RHEMA Bible Training College (1974) — 80,000+ graduates in 52 countries.

↓

**Word of Faith movement** — Kenneth Copeland, Benny Hinn, Joyce Meyer, Creflo Dollar, Joel Osteen. The doctrine spread globally through RHEMA graduates planting churches.

### The Critical Distinction: MacMillan vs. Kenyon

John MacMillan (CMA missionary, 1932) wrote the original orthodox "Authority of the Believer" — teaching delegated authority through positional truth in Ephesians 1-2, requiring consecration and obedience, operating within God's sovereign will, focused on missionary spiritual warfare.

Kenyon taught something entirely different: autonomous authority wielded by believers through "positive confession," faith as a "force," and words as "containers" of creative power.

Hagin plagiarized BOTH, blending MacMillan's orthodox framework with Kenyon's heterodox innovations. The result looked biblical on the surface (Ephesians language, positional truth) but carried New Thought DNA underneath (faith as force, words creating reality, believers as gods).

MacMillan scholar Paul L. King confirms: MacMillan "would not approve of all that is currently taught...such as 'little gods' and 'name it and claim it.'"

### Was It EVER Taught Before Kenyon?

**No.** No church father, no Reformer, no pre-1890 Protestant taught that believers possess inherent spiritual authority, that faith is a "force," that spoken words create physical reality, or that Jesus died spiritually and was "born again."

What historic Christianity DID teach:
- Authority over demons through Christ's delegated power and prayer (early church fathers)
- Spiritual warfare through consecration and intercession (Keswick/Higher Life tradition)
- Positional truth in Ephesians as the basis for prayer and spiritual confidence (MacMillan, 1932)

None of this resembles the Word of Faith version.
