# What Does the Scapegoat Represent on the Day of Atonement?

## Question

What does the scapegoat represent on the Day of Atonement?

## Summary Answer

The live goat is an object acted upon, not an atoning agent. **Leviticus 16:10 makes atonement OVER the live goat (the same construction used of the altar in 16:18), never BY it.** This grammatical conclusion follows necessarily from the text. Functionally, **the live-goat rite removes and carries away confessed sin after blood-atonement is finished, rather than making atonement itself.** This too is a necessary inference from grammar, ritual sequence, sin-bearing register, and the day's stated effect. The one genuinely open question is lexical: **The word 'Azazel' underdetermines its own class — being, place, or common noun for 'the goat that departs' — and the text leaves it unresolved.** This material uncertainty is held at low confidence and does not weaken the stronger conclusions. On the referent, the scapegoat is not Christ (necessary inference) and corresponds at most to Satan as a judicial, non-redemptive figure (calibrated reasonable inference).

## Key Verses

> **The two goats and the lots (Leviticus 16:8-10)** And Aaron shall cast lots upon the two goats; one lot for the LORD, and the other lot for the scapegoat. And Aaron shall bring the goat upon which the LORD'S lot fell, and offer him for a sin offering. But the goat, on which the lot fell to be the scapegoat, shall be presented alive before the LORD, to make an atonement with him, and to let him go for a scapegoat into the wilderness.

> **The LORD's goat killed (Leviticus 16:15)** Then shall he kill the goat of the sin offering, that is for the people, and bring his blood within the vail, and do with that blood as he did with the blood of the bullock, and sprinkle it upon the mercy seat, and before the mercy seat:

> **Atonement over the altar (Leviticus 16:18)** And he shall go out unto the altar that is before the LORD, and make an atonement for it; and shall take of the blood of the bullock, and of the blood of the goat, and put it upon the horns of the altar round about.

> **Atonement completed before the live goat (Leviticus 16:20)** And when he hath made an end of reconciling the holy place, and the tabernacle of the congregation, and the altar, he shall bring the live goat:

> **Confession and imputation (Leviticus 16:21)** And Aaron shall lay both his hands upon the head of the live goat, and confess over him all the iniquities of the children of Israel, and all their transgressions in all their sins, putting them upon the head of the goat, and shall send him away by the hand of a fit man into the wilderness:

> **The goat bears sin to a solitary land (Leviticus 16:22)** And the goat shall bear upon him all their iniquities unto a land not inhabited: and he shall let go the goat in the wilderness.

> **The day cleanses (Leviticus 16:30)** For on that day shall the priest make an atonement for you, to cleanse you, that ye may be clean from all your sins before the LORD.

## Analysis

### Step 1: The two goats belong to one occasion but perform two different acts

The Day of Atonement opens with two goats taken "for a sin offering" and one ram for a burnt offering (Lev 16:5). The phrase "sin offering" is a single Hebrew word, *chattat*, governing the pair. The Christ-view builds on this, arguing that because both goats are one sin offering, both share one function and both point to Christ. That inference fails on the text itself. The same chapter assigns the two goats acts that cannot both belong to one animal: one is killed and its blood brought inside the veil (16:9, 15), the other is presented alive and sent away (16:10, 21-22). The word *chattat* names both the offense and the sin-offering complex; it is a category label, not a description of a shared act. Because Leviticus 16 unavoidably prescribes incompatible acts for the two goats, reading "one sin offering" as one function would contradict the ritual the text prescribes. This is a necessary inference, and it removes the foundation of the "one sin offering unifies the goats" argument before the rest of the case is even made.

The lots divide the two goats (16:8). One lot falls "for the LORD," the other "for the scapegoat," the disputed word Azazel. The LORD's-lot goat is offered as a sin offering. The other is "presented alive before the LORD." From the very first act, the ritual separates a goat that will die from a goat that will live and be sent away. Everything that follows develops that division.

### Step 2: Atonement is made over the live goat, not by it

Leviticus 16:10 is the crux of the whole question. The live goat "shall be presented alive before the LORD, to make an atonement with him." The Hebrew phrase is *lekapper alav*, "to make atonement over/upon it," the verb *kaphar* with the preposition *al*. The goat is "presented alive" by the verb *yaomad*, a passive form meaning "is made to stand." So the goat is passive, an object made to stand, and the atonement is made over it.

The decisive control is that this same construction, *kaphar* plus *al*, lands on the altar in 16:18: "make an atonement for it," where "it" is the altar. The altar is plainly an inanimate object being purged, not a co-atoner. The same construction reaches the holy place in 16:16. When Aaron makes atonement for himself and the people, by contrast, the text switches to a different preposition, *ba'ad*, "on behalf of" (16:17). That form marks the beneficiaries for whom atonement is made. The live goat never takes *ba'ad*; it takes *al*, the object marker. **Leviticus 16:10 makes atonement OVER the live goat (the same construction used of the altar in 16:18), never BY it.** This is the grammatical_object axis, and it holds at necessary-inference strength: the al-preposition and the passive presentation together mark the goat as an object acted upon, governed by send (*shalach*), bear (*nasa*), and impute (*natan al-rosh*), while slaughter (*shachat*) is reserved for the LORD's goat alone.

This forecloses the Christ-view's strongest grammatical claim. That view presses the presence of an atonement word next to the live goat in 16:10. But the disposal reading does not rest on the mere absence of *kaphar*; it rests on the preposition and the completion boundary. An atonement word does stand near the live goat, which is exactly why the point has to be made grammatically rather than by silence.

### Step 3: Blood-atonement is finished before the live goat is handled

The ritual is sequenced, and the sequence is an argument. Aaron first atones with his own bullock (16:11), then kills the LORD's-lot goat and brings its blood within the veil (16:15), then purges the holy place and the altar with blood (16:16-19). Then comes the hinge verse: "when he hath made an end of reconciling the holy place, and the tabernacle, and the altar, he shall bring the live goat" (16:20). The Hebrew is *vekhillah mikapper*, the completion verb *kalah* meaning "he has finished atoning." Only after that is the live goat "brought," and the verb is *hiqriv*, "bring near," not "offer up."

The ordered phases are therefore three: cleanse by blood (16:14-19), complete atonement (16:20), then dispose of sin via the live goat (16:21-22). The live-goat rite is explicitly sequenced after the completion of blood-atonement. It is a distinct terminal act, not a part of the propitiation. This is an explicit reading of the text, since 16:20 states the completion directly. Aaron's garment change and further burnt offerings (16:23-24) frame the live-goat dispatch as its own bounded segment, reinforcing that it stands apart from the blood work.

### Step 4: The live goat sheds no blood, so it cannot propitiate

Only the LORD's goat is slain. The verb is *shachat* (16:15), the slaughter verb, and its blood is brought within the veil. The live goat is never slain; it is presented alive (16:10). Leviticus 17:11 states the principle that atonement runs on blood: "it is the blood that maketh an atonement for the soul." Hebrews 9:22 states the same rule for the new covenant: "without shedding of blood is no remission." A figure that sheds no blood cannot be a blood-propitiation. This is a necessary inference. The author directly labels the blood-atonement of the LORD's goat and never gives that vocabulary to the live goat, and Hebrews supplies the controlling principle that a bloodless figure cannot remit sin.

### Step 5: The goat bears sin to a place, in the removal register

Leviticus 16:22 states what the live goat does: "the goat shall bear upon him all their iniquities unto a land not inhabited: and he shall let go the goat in the wilderness." The verb "bear" is *nasa*. The destination is *erets gezerah*, "a land of separation," a hapax phrase occurring only here. Sin is carried away from the community to an empty place. There is no offering-verb directed at any being there; the stated goal is a place, not a recipient. Before this, in 16:21, Aaron lays both hands on the goat, confesses the full sin triad — iniquity (*avon*), transgression (*pesha*), and sin (*chattat*) — and puts them on the goat's head. Sin is placed on the goat by confession, imputed onto a bearer, not borne by voluntary self-offering. The goat neither consents nor intercedes.

Taken together, grammar, sequence, and this removal register converge. **The live-goat rite removes and carries away confessed sin after blood-atonement is finished, rather than making atonement itself.** This is the functional_conceptual_identity axis, and it holds at necessary-inference strength.

### Step 6: The day cleanses; it does not pronounce individual forgiveness

Leviticus 16:30 states the day's effect: "on that day shall the priest make an atonement for you, to cleanse you." The verbs move from *kaphar* (make atonement) to *taher* (cleanse). What is missing is just as telling. The daily sin offering ends with forgiveness: "the priest shall make an atonement for them, and it shall be forgiven them" (Lev 4:20), the verb *salach*. That forgiveness verb never appears anywhere in Leviticus 16. The Day of Atonement cleanses the sanctuary and the people corporately (16:33-34, "once a year"); it does not pronounce individual pardon. The effect-formula frames the live-goat rite as disposal within a cleansing frame, not a forgiveness pronouncement. This closes the four-strand functional case: grammar (atonement over the goat), sequence (completion before the goat), register (bearing to a place), and effect (cleansing, forgiveness absent) all point to terminal disposal of already-atoned confessed sin.

**The scapegoat most directly represents the complete removal of confessed sin, carried away after atonement is finished.** This is the established functional referent at necessary-inference strength. The live goat is a terminal disposal vehicle that carries confessed iniquity away to an uninhabited land after atonement is completed, and the act is a sending toward a place, not an offering.

### Step 7: The verb "bear" is neutral, so direction decides the referent

The referent question turns on the verb *nasa*, "bear/carry," because the same verb is used of Christ's sin-bearing. But *nasa* is register-neutral. When God is the subject, *nasa* means to forgive: "forgiving iniquity and transgression and sin" (Exo 34:7), and the three-term inventory there matches the triad confessed over the scapegoat. When the priest is the subject, *nasa* means to bear iniquity Godward for acceptance (Exo 28:38; Lev 10:17). When the Servant is the subject, *nasa* is redemptive and paired with intercession (Isa 53:11-12), justifying many. And when the scapegoat is the subject, *nasa* means to carry sin away to the wilderness (Lev 16:22). One verb spans divine forgiveness, priestly bearing, redemptive bearing, and removal. The bare verb cannot decide identity.

Direction decides. The scapegoat carries sin wilderness-ward, away from the community and away from God's presence. The New Testament fixes Christ's sin-bearing the opposite direction. Christ was "offered to bear the sins of many" (Heb 9:28), the verb *anaphero*, whose prefix marks upward, Godward motion; he "bare our sins in his own body on the tree" (1 Pet 2:24), again *anaphero*; and he is "the Lamb of God, which taketh away the sin of the world" (John 1:29), the verb *airo*. That last point matters, because *airo* is the very Septuagint equivalent of Hebrew *nasa*. The shared verb between the scapegoat and Christ is real, and it is neutralized not by a different word but by the opposite direction. Christ bears sin Godward and Lamb-ward; the scapegoat carries it away.

### Step 8: Christ occupies the register the live goat is excluded from

The not-Christ verdict does not rest only on the direction of bearing; it rests on where the New Testament positively locates Christ. Christ enters "by his own blood... having obtained eternal redemption" (Heb 9:11-12). He appears "in the presence of God for us" and "put away sin by the sacrifice of himself" (Heb 9:24-26), Godward, sacrificial, once-for-all. God set him forth as a "propitiation through faith in his blood" (Rom 3:24-26), where "propitiation" is *hilasterion*, the Septuagint word for the mercy seat, the blood-focus of the Day of Atonement. Christ occupies the slain-goat, blood, mercy-seat, and high-priest register. That is precisely the register the bloodless, wilderness-ward live goat is excluded from. **The live goat contributes nothing to salvation: bloodless and never slain, it carries off already-atoned sin rather than redeeming.** Redemption belongs to the blood-borne LORD's goat and the high priest, that is, to Christ, not to the scapegoat. This is the redemptive_action axis at necessary-inference strength.

**The scapegoat is not a second symbol of Christ: it sheds no blood, is never slain, and bears sin away from God rather than toward Him.** The live goat is bloodless (presented alive, 16:10; Heb 9:22 forecloses remission without blood), is never slain, is atoned-over rather than atoning, and bears sin the opposite direction from Christ. The one New Testament release-scene that might echo the two-goat pattern inverts it. In the Barabbas episode (Mat 27:15-26), the guilty party is freed and the innocent is condemned, the reverse of Leviticus 16 where both goats are innocent and the LORD's goat dies. That inversion supplies no route mapping the scapegoat onto Christ. On this evidence the Christ-view is eliminated at necessary-inference strength.

### Step 9: "For Azazel" is a destination, not a recipient of sacrifice

Some readings treat "for Azazel" as marking a being to whom the goat is offered, a demon appeased by the rite. This fails at the level of syntax. Leviticus 16 uses only send (*shalach*) and bear (*nasa*) for the live goat, and the handler is defiled and must wash (16:26), a contamination-transfer signal. It never uses a sacrifice-verb. The Torah does have vocabulary for sacrificing to a wilderness goat-being: Leviticus 17:7, immediately after the Day of Atonement chapter, forbids Israel to "offer their sacrifices unto devils," the *se'irim*, using the sacrifice-verb *zabach* with a goat-being as recipient. That verb is exactly what is absent from the Azazel rite. So the author's own vocabulary for offering to a goat-being is conspicuously withheld from the live goat.

**Leviticus 16 never sacrifices the goat to a wilderness being: it is sent, not offered, so 'for Azazel' marks a destination, not a recipient of sacrifice.** In 16:10 *la-azazel* stands beside *ha-midbarah*, "for Azazel, wilderness-ward," where the directional ending marks a goal. The same string is used directionally, which shows Azazel functioning as the destination the goat is sent toward. Whether Azazel is even a wilderness being at all remains an open material question, but no sacrifice is offered to it. The demon-destination-as-sacrifice reading is eliminated.

### Step 10: The word "Azazel" is genuinely underdetermined

The remaining open question is what the word Azazel actually denotes. It occurs only four times, all in Leviticus 16. Two lines of evidence pull in opposite directions and neither forecloses the other. On one side, the parser reads Azazel as a proper noun, and the 16:8 parallel *la-YHWH* // *la-azazel* is the only place in Scripture where a non-divine term takes the same preposition-plus-noun framing as the divine name. That pressures a personal reading. On the other side, the compound etymology *ez* plus *azal* yields "goat of departure," the Septuagint renders it descriptively as *apopompaios* ("one sent away"), and the same *lamed* marks a bare destination in 16:10. That keeps the common-noun and place readings live.

**The word 'Azazel' underdetermines its own class — being, place, or common noun for 'the goat that departs' — and the text leaves it unresolved.** This is the material_subtype axis, held at low confidence, honestly reported as uncertain. The one datum that breaks any would-be proof is intra-textual: the same *la-azazel* is used directionally in 16:10, so the 16:8 parallel cannot force a personal recipient. Crucially, this uncertainty is quarantined. The grammatical apposition of *la-azazel* to *ha-midbarah* separates what the goat does from the destination or word Azazel. The lexical class of the word cannot reopen the settled functional and referent conclusions. Uncertainty about the word does not weaken the conclusions about the act.

### Step 11: The Satan correspondence, calibrated and bounded

Once Christ is eliminated and the demon-sacrifice reading defeated, the surviving referent question is whether the scapegoat corresponds to Satan. It does, but only as a judicial, non-redemptive figure, and the language must stay "corresponds to," never "is." Leviticus 16 never says Azazel is Satan. The adversary is named only in other texts: Satan the accuser before the LORD (Zec 3:1-2), the devil who "sinneth from the beginning" (1 John 3:8), and the serpent under enmity (Gen 3:15). The structural correspondence comes from Revelation 20:1-3, where Satan is bound alive by a nameless angelic agent and confined to the abyss after the completed work of the cross. That parallels the live-goat rite: a living, unslain, sin-associated figure removed by a designated agent to a desolate place after atonement is completed.

**The live goat corresponds to Satan only judicially and partially — as the one held accountable for sin and removed — never as a redeemer, and Leviticus 16 never names him.** The correspondence is real but partial. Revelation 20's "little season" release (20:3, 7) and the lake-of-fire destruction (20:10) exceed the type: the scapegoat is sent alive and the narrative simply ends. The abyss is not lexically the wilderness, and Revelation 20 never cites Leviticus 16. So the correspondence is carried at reasonable-inference strength, weaker than the not-Christ verdict, and it remains judicial. Any accountability laid on the adversary is a matter of removal, never a contribution to salvation. Galatians 3:13 ("made a curse for us") shows that facets of Christ's sin-dealing exceed the two-goat framework, which caps how far the correspondence in either direction may be pressed.

### Bringing the axes together

The integrated answer holds on three independent axes. Grammatically, atonement is made over the live goat, not by it. Functionally, the rite removes and carries away confessed sin after blood-atonement is finished. On the referent, the goat is not Christ and corresponds only judicially to Satan. The lexical class of the word Azazel is the one open matter, honestly reported as unresolved and quarantined so it never reopens the settled axes. The functional and referent conclusions stand at necessary-inference strength on their own evidence; the material uncertainty is carried and reported, never allowed to weaken them.

## Word Studies

Several original-language findings carry the argument. *Kaphar* (H3722, "atone/cover," 130 occurrences) with the preposition *al* marks the object atonement is made over. In 16:10 (*lekapper alav*) it applies to the live goat exactly as it applies to the altar in 16:18, an inanimate object. The contrast with *ba'ad* ("on behalf of") in 16:17 shows the live goat is not a beneficiary but an object acted upon.

*Kalah* (H3615, "complete/finish," 258 occurrences) in 16:20 marks blood-atonement as an accomplished end before the live goat is brought forward, fixing the live-goat rite as a later phase.

*Nasa* (H5375, "bear/carry," 759 occurrences) is the pivot of the referent question. Its range covers divine forgiveness (Exo 34:7), priestly bearing (Exo 28:38), redemptive Servant-bearing (Isa 53:11-12), and wilderness-ward removal (Lev 16:22). Because the verb is neutral, direction decides identity. The Greek verbs of Christ's sin-bearing, *anaphero* (G399, 12 occurrences) and *airo* (G142, 110 occurrences), both point Godward or through the Lamb's death, opposite the goat's direction. That *airo* is the standard Septuagint rendering of *nasa* makes the verb overlap genuine and the direction-based distinction essential.

*Shachat* (H7819, "slaughter," 93 occurrences) governs only the LORD's goat, never the live one, marking the blood-atonement register the live goat is excluded from. *Shalach* (H7971, "send away") and *natan* (H5414, "put/place," in the *natan al-rosh* imputation gesture) govern the live goat, marking dispatch and imputation, not offering.

*Azazel* (H5799) occurs only four times, all in Leviticus 16. It parses as a proper noun, but the etymology *ez* plus *azal* ("goat of departure"), the Septuagint's descriptive rendering, and the destination use of the same *lamed* keep the common-noun and place readings live. The destination *erets gezerah* rests on *gezerah* (H1509), a hapax occurring only in 16:22, "a land of separation." The handler is an *ish itti* (H6261), also a hapax, "a fit man," a nameless functional agent rather than a priestly title. The day's effect verb *taher* (H2891, "cleanse," 115 occurrences) issues from *kaphar* in 16:30, while the forgiveness verb *salach* (H5545) is entirely absent from Leviticus 16, unlike the daily rite of Lev 4:20.

## Difficult Passages

### Hebrews 13:11-13 — "Outside the camp"

This passage is advanced as evidence that the scapegoat maps onto Christ, since the live goat too is sent outside the camp. The tension is genuine, because the live goat is indeed sent out of the camp. But Hebrews 13:11 specifies which animals it means: "the bodies of those beasts, whose blood is brought into the sanctuary by the high priest for sin, are burned without the camp." That describes the slain sin-offering carcasses of Leviticus 16:27, the bullock and the LORD's goat, whose blood was brought inside. It does not describe the live goat, which sheds no blood and whose blood never enters the sanctuary.

The prior study atone-03-outside-the-gate settles this directly: it establishes through the "blood inside means body outside" rule (Lev 6:30; 10:16-18) that Hebrews 13:11's markers — "by the high priest" and "brought into the sanctuary" — identify the slain Day-of-Atonement sin-offering body of Leviticus 16:27, not the live goat. The prior study atone-19-sin-path-two-goats likewise traced this distinction and settled that the two "outside the camp" motions are different rites: the slain-carcass disposal of 16:27 and the live-goat banishment of 16:21-22. The prior study doa-two-goats-referents likewise kept the two goats in separate registers. Applying those findings here narrows the tension to a category error. The Christ-view conflates the slain goat's carcass disposal with the live goat's banishment. Hebrews' type runs from the slain body burned outside the camp to Jesus' body suffering outside the gate, both marked by shed blood brought into the sanctuary. So Hebrews 13:11-13 supports Christ as the LORD's goat and high priest, not as the scapegoat. The passage corroborates the study's finding rather than undermining it. The ancient two-goat Christ typology (Barnabas 7; Justin, Dialogue 40) read the scapegoat as Christ, but that is patristic interpretation, not what Leviticus 16 states, and it rests on the same conflation of two distinct rites.

### Revelation 20:1-3 — The correspondence and where it exceeds the type

The Satan referent rests on a structural correspondence between the live-goat rite (Lev 16:20-22) and Satan bound (Rev 20:1-3). Both occur after completed atonement or judgment, both involve a living creature confined alive, both involve a nameless functional agent, and both involve a desolate destination. The prior studies rev-20-1-3-scapegoat-day-of-atonement and rev-20-scapegoat-satan-bound established this six-element removal-phase parallel and are the basis for the calibrated correspondence used here. But Revelation 20 goes further than the type. The scapegoat is let go in the wilderness and never returns, whereas Satan is "loosed a little season" (20:3, 7) and then destroyed in the lake of fire (20:10). The abyss (*abyssos*) is not lexically the wilderness (*midbar* / *eremos*), and Revelation 20 never cites Leviticus 16.

Where the antitype exceeds the type at these points, the correspondence must be stated at reasonable-inference strength as "corresponds to," never as a direct "Azazel is Satan" identification, and it must not be read back to over-determine Leviticus 16. The prior Rev-20 studies drew exactly this boundary: the removal phase is a real parallel, but the release and destruction have no counterpart in the scapegoat rite. This is why the adversary material stays anchored in the naming texts (Zec 3:1-2; 1 John 3:8; Gen 3:15), and why the Satan correspondence is carried at a weaker strength than the not-Christ verdict. The correspondence is judicial and non-redemptive throughout. Christ alone atones and redeems (Heb 9:11-12; Rom 3:25; John 1:29).

## Conclusion

The scapegoat on the Day of Atonement represents the complete removal of confessed sin, carried away after blood-atonement is already finished. This conclusion stands on four independent strands that converge at necessary-inference strength. Grammatically, atonement is made over the live goat, using the same construction applied to the altar in 16:18, not by it (Lev 16:10, 16:18). By sequence, blood-atonement is explicitly completed before the live goat is even brought forward (Lev 16:20). By register, the goat bears sin away to a solitary land, a removal aimed at a place (Lev 16:22). By effect, the day cleanses corporately, with individual forgiveness conspicuously absent (Lev 16:30; contrast Lev 4:20). The live goat sheds no blood and is never slain, so it cannot propitiate (Lev 16:15; 17:11; Heb 9:22).

The scapegoat is not Christ. This is a necessary inference. Christ bears sin the opposite direction, Godward and Lamb-ward (Heb 9:28; John 1:29), and occupies the blood and mercy-seat register the live goat is excluded from (Rom 3:24-26; Heb 9:11-12). Stated as the referent verdict: The scapegoat is not Christ; it corresponds at most to Satan as a judicial figure receiving accountability, never as a redeemer. The scapegoat corresponds to Satan only as a judicial, non-redemptive figure, a calibrated reasonable inference, since Leviticus 16 never names him, the adversary is identified only in other texts (Zec 3:1-2; 1 John 3:8; Gen 3:15), and Revelation 20's release and destruction exceed the type (Rev 20:3, 10). The demon-destination-as-sacrifice reading is defeated at the level of syntax, because Leviticus 16 sends the goat and never sacrifices it to any being (Lev 16:10, 22; contrast Lev 17:7). The one genuinely open matter is what the word Azazel denotes, which the text underdetermines, held at low confidence and quarantined so it never weakens the functional and referent conclusions.
