# Verses

## Question
What is the biblical connection between the flood of Noah and the final fire judgment? Does Scripture itself make this connection explicitly?

### Scoffers deny future judgment — Peter's refutation rests on the flood precedent

> 3 Knowing this first, that there shall come in the last days scoffers, walking after their own lusts, 4 And saying, Where is the promise of his coming? for since the fathers fell asleep, all things continue as [they were] from the beginning of the creation.

**Context:** Peter's second epistle, chapter 3. Peter addresses last-days scoffers who deny Christ's return and future judgment by appealing to uniformitarianism — 'all things continue as they were.' This sets up Peter's counter-argument from the flood in vv.5-7.

**Notes:** The scoffers' argument assumes continuity ('all things continue'). Peter will refute this by pointing to the flood as a precedent for divine interruption of the natural order. The phrase 'from the beginning of the creation' echoes Gen 1:1 and establishes the creation baseline that Peter's argument will track through three stages.

### PRIMARY TEXT — Peter's explicit three-stage argument: creation by word, world perished by water, present heavens/earth reserved for fire by same word

> 5 For this they willingly are ignorant of, that by the word of God the heavens were of old, and the earth standing out of the water and in the water: 6 Whereby the world that then was, being overflowed with water, perished: 7 But the heavens and the earth, which are now, by the same word are kept in store, reserved unto fire against the day of judgment and perdition of ungodly men.

**Context:** The heart of Peter's argument in 2 Peter 3. Peter constructs a three-stage typological framework: (1) creation by divine word (v.5), (2) world destroyed by water via that same word (v.6), (3) present heavens and earth reserved by the same word for fire judgment (v.7). This is the single most explicit biblical connection between the flood and fire judgment.

### Peter's conclusion: the Lord knows how to reserve the unjust for judgment

> The Lord knoweth how to deliver the godly out of temptations, and to reserve the unjust unto the day of judgment to be punished:

**Context:** The conclusion drawn from the flood/Sodom examples. terein (G5083, to keep/reserve) appears again — the same verb used in 3:7 for the present heavens and earth 'reserved unto fire.'

**Notes:** The dual pattern — deliver the righteous, reserve the wicked — holds across all three examples (angels, flood, Sodom) and extends to the eschatological fire judgment. The verb tereo (G5083) links 2:9 to 3:7.

### Jesus explicitly pairs days of Noah (flood) with days of Lot (fire) as dual types of Son of Man's coming

> 26 And as it was in the days of Noe, so shall it be also in the days of the Son of man. 27 They did eat, they drank, they married wives, they were given in marriage, until the day that Noe entered into the ark, and the flood came, and destroyed them all. 28 Likewise also as it was in the days of Lot; they did eat, they drank, they bought, they sold, they planted, they builded; 29 But the same day that Lot went out of Sodom it rained fire and brimstone from heaven, and destroyed [them] all. 30 Even thus shall it be in the day when the Son of man is revealed.

**Context:** Luke 17:20-37 — Jesus teaches about the coming of the kingdom and the day of the Son of Man. He pairs two OT judgments as dual types: Noah's flood (water) and Lot's Sodom (fire). The structural parallelism is exact — identical formula for both: mundane activities, then sudden destruction, then 'destroyed them all.'

**Notes:** CRITICAL: v.27 — apolesen pantas (G622, aorist active, 'destroyed them all') for the FLOOD. v.29 — apolesen pantas (identical form, G622 aorist active, 'destroyed them all') for SODOM'S FIRE. Jesus uses the exact same verb and form for both judgments. v.30 — 'Even thus shall it be' (kata ta auta) — explicitly typological application. The 'likewise also' (homoios kai) in v.28 formally links the two. This is the second strongest explicit biblical connection between flood and fire judgment. Unique to Luke — Matthew 24:37-39 has only the Noah portion.

### Days of Noah parallel — ordinary life then sudden destruction; the flood took them all away

> 37 But as the days of Noe [were], so shall also the coming of the Son of man be. 38 For as in the days that were before the flood they were eating and drinking, marrying and giving in marriage, until the day that Noe entered into the ark, 39 And knew not until the flood came, and took them all away; so shall also the coming of the Son of man be.

**Context:** Matthew 24 — Olivet Discourse. Jesus uses only the Noah/flood type here (no Lot/fire). The emphasis is on 'knew not' (ouk egnosan) — oblivious unpreparedness — rather than the medium of destruction.

**Notes:** v.39 — eren hapantas (G142, aorist active, 'took them all away') — Matthew uses airo (take away) rather than Luke's apollymi (destroy). kataklusmos (G2627) appears for 'the flood' — the technical Greek term. parousia (G3952) in v.39 — 'the coming of the Son of man.' Matthew's version does not explicitly add the fire dimension — that pairing is unique to Luke 17.

### Universal moral corruption as cause of flood — every imagination only evil continually

> 5 And GOD saw that the wickedness of man [was] great in the earth, and [that] every imagination of the thoughts of his heart [was] only evil continually. 6 And it repented the LORD that he had made man on the earth, and it grieved him at his heart. 7 And the LORD said, I will destroy man whom I have created from the face of the earth; both man, and beast, and the creeping thing, and the fowls of the air; for it repenteth me that I have made them.

**Context:** Genesis 6 — The moral cause of the flood. Universal corruption of the human race. God's grief and resolve to destroy.

**Notes:** The universality of pre-flood corruption ('every imagination... only evil continually') establishes the pattern that universal moral corruption warrants universal judgment. Peter picks this up in 2 Pet 2:5 ('the world of the ungodly') and applies it to the final fire judgment.

### God's announcement of the flood — 'I will destroy them with the earth'

> 13 And God said unto Noah, The end of all flesh is come before me; for the earth is filled with violence through them; and, behold, I will destroy them with the earth. ... 17 And, behold, I, even I, do bring a flood of waters upon the earth, to destroy all flesh, wherein [is] the breath of life, from under heaven; [and] every thing that [is] in the earth shall die.

**Context:** Genesis 6 — God announces the flood judgment to Noah. mashchitham (H7843, Hiphil participle + 3mp suffix, 'I am destroying them') uses the same root shachath that described the earth's corruption (v.11-12), creating a wordplay: the corrupt (shachath) earth is destroyed (shachath).

**Notes:** v.13 — mashchitham et-ha'arets ('I am destroying them with the earth') — shachath (H7843) in Hiphil. The same verb is used for the pre-flood corruption (v.11-12 Niphal/Hiphil). The wordplay is deliberate: the corruption (shachath) of the earth leads to the destruction (shachath) of the earth. v.17 — mabbul (H3999) appears — the specific Hebrew term for Noah's flood, used only 13 times in the OT.

### Flood mechanism — fountains of the deep + windows of heaven (water from above and below)

> 11 In the six hundredth year of Noah's life, in the second month, the seventeenth day of the month, the same day were all the fountains of the great deep broken up, and the windows of heaven were opened. 12 And the rain was upon the earth forty days and forty nights.

**Context:** Genesis 7 — The flood mechanism involves water from two sources: fountains of the great deep (below) and windows of heaven (above). This dual-source mechanism is parallel to what EGW elaborates about fire judgment (fire from heaven + fire from the earth), though the biblical text itself does not explicitly develop this parallel for fire.

**Notes:** The dual-source water mechanism (above + below) is an observation that EGW develops into a parallel with fire judgment, but Scripture does not explicitly draw this connection. Rev 20:9 mentions 'fire came down from God out of heaven' but no parallel 'fire from below' text. This is an EGW elaboration beyond the biblical text.

### Universal scope of flood destruction — all flesh died

> 21 And all flesh died that moved upon the earth, both of fowl, and of cattle, and of beast, and of every creeping thing that creepeth upon the earth, and every man: 22 All in whose nostrils [was] the breath of life, of all that [was] in the dry [land], died. 23 And every living substance was destroyed which was upon the face of the ground, both man, and cattle, and the creeping things, and the fowl of the heaven; and they were destroyed from the earth: and Noah only remained [alive], and they that [were] with him in the ark.

**Context:** The universal scope of the flood destruction — all flesh, all creatures, every man. Only Noah and those in the ark survived. This universality is what Peter's argument in 2 Pet 3:5-7 requires: the scope of past water judgment validates the scope of future fire judgment.

**Notes:** Davidson argues Peter's flood typology 'assumes and depends upon' the universality of the flood. This text establishes that universality: 'all flesh died,' 'Noah only remained alive.' Peter uses kosmos (world) in 2 Pet 3:6 for the scope, matching the universal language here.

### Noahic covenant and rainbow — God promises no more flood, implicitly leaving room for different judgment

> 8 And God spake unto Noah, and to his sons with him, saying, 9 And I, behold, I establish my covenant with you, and with your seed after you; 10 And with every living creature that [is] with you, of the fowl, of the cattle, and of every beast of the earth with you; from all that go out of the ark, to every beast of the earth. 11 And I will establish my covenant with you; neither shall all flesh be cut off any more by the waters of a flood; neither shall there any more be a flood to destroy the earth. 12 And God said, This [is] the token of the covenant which I make between me and you and every living creature that [is] with you, for perpetual generations: 13 I do set my bow in the cloud, and it shall be for a token of a covenant between me and the earth. 14 And it shall come to pass, when I bring a cloud over the earth, that the bow shall be seen in the cloud: 15 And I will remember my covenant, which [is] between me and you and every living creature of all flesh; and the waters shall no more become a flood to destroy all flesh. 16 And the bow shall be in the cloud; and I will look upon it, that I may remember the everlasting covenant between God and every living creature of all flesh that [is] upon the earth.

**Context:** Genesis 9 — God's covenant with Noah. The promise is specifically about water: 'neither shall there any more be a flood (mabbul) to destroy the earth.' The covenant excludes future water destruction but does not address fire.

**Notes:** v.11 — mimmey hammabbul ('by the waters of a flood') + mabbul leshacheth ha'arets ('a flood to destroy the earth'). The specificity is critical: the covenant promise is limited to water destruction. Peter may be building on this logic in 2 Pet 3:5-7 — God promised no more water judgment, but by the same divine word, fire judgment is reserved. The covenant does not preclude a different mode of judgment.

### Sodom destruction — fire and brimstone rained from the LORD out of heaven

> 24 Then the LORD rained upon Sodom and upon Gomorrah brimstone and fire from the LORD out of heaven; 25 And he overthrew those cities, and all the plain, and all the inhabitants of the cities, and that which grew upon the ground.

**Context:** Genesis 19 — The destruction of Sodom and Gomorrah by fire and brimstone from heaven. This becomes the paradigmatic fire judgment in Scripture — referenced by Jesus (Luke 17:29), Peter (2 Pet 2:6), Jude (Jude 1:7), and throughout the prophets.

**Notes:** gophriyth (H1614, brimstone) + esh (H784, fire) — the Sodom formula. This exact pairing recurs in prophetic and apocalyptic judgment passages. Jesus quotes this directly in Luke 17:29 (ebrexen pyr kai theion ap' ouranou). Jude calls it 'the vengeance of eternal fire' (Jude 1:7). Peter calls it an 'ensample' (hupodeigma, 2 Pet 2:6).

### Abraham sees smoke ascending as the smoke of a furnace — echoed in Revelation

> And he looked toward Sodom and Gomorrah, and toward all the land of the plain, and beheld, and, lo, the smoke of the country went up as the smoke of a furnace.

**Context:** Abraham observes the aftermath of Sodom's destruction. The 'smoke ascending as the smoke of a furnace' formula is echoed in Rev 14:11 ('the smoke of their torment ascendeth up'), Rev 19:3 ('her smoke rose up for ever'), and Isa 34:10 ('the smoke thereof shall go up for ever').

**Notes:** The smoke-ascending formula communicates magnitude and completeness of destruction, not duration of burning. Isaiah 34:10 uses the same image for Edom — 'smoke shall go up for ever' — yet Edom is not still burning (animals dwell in the ruins, Isa 34:11-17).

### Enoch's prophecy of the Lord coming to execute judgment — pre-flood judgment warning

> 14 And Enoch also, the seventh from Adam, prophesied of these, saying, Behold, the Lord cometh with ten thousands of his saints, 15 To execute judgment upon all, and to convince all that are ungodly among them of all their ungodly deeds which they have ungodly committed, and of all their hard [speeches] which ungodly sinners have spoken against him.

**Context:** Jude cites Enoch — a pre-flood prophet — warning of eschatological judgment. This connects the pre-flood era directly to final judgment, reinforcing the flood-to-fire typological framework.

**Notes:** Enoch lived before the flood and prophesied judgment. Jude applies this prophecy eschatologically. The fourfold repetition of 'ungodly' (asebes/asebeia) in v.15 mirrors Peter's 'world of the ungodly' (kosmos asebon) in 2 Pet 2:5 and 'perdition of ungodly men' (asebon anthropon) in 2 Pet 3:7.

### Original creation — heavens, earth, Spirit moving on waters; baseline for uncreation/re-creation pattern

> 1 In the beginning God created the heaven and the earth. 2 And the earth was without form, and void; and darkness [was] upon the face of the deep. And the Spirit of God moved upon the face of the waters.

**Context:** Genesis 1 — The original creation. This is the baseline against which both the flood (uncreation) and the fire judgment (final uncreation) are measured. Peter's argument in 2 Pet 3:5 starts here: 'by the word of God the heavens were of old.'

**Notes:** v.1 — shamayim (H8064) + erets (H776) — heavens and earth, the same pair Peter uses in 2 Pet 3:5 (ouranoi + ge) and 3:7 (hoi nyn ouranoi kai he ge) and 3:13 (kainous ouranous kai gen kainen). v.2 — ruach elohim merachepheth al-pene hammayim — Spirit/wind of God hovering over face of the waters. Paralleled in Gen 8:1 where ruach passes over the earth in post-flood re-creation.

### Heavens perish, earth waxes old like garment; God endures — cosmic impermanence vs. divine permanence

> 25 Of old hast thou laid the foundation of the earth: and the heavens [are] the work of thy hands. 26 They shall perish, but thou shalt endure: yea, all of them shall wax old like a garment; as a vesture shalt thou change them, and they shall be changed: 27 But thou [art] the same, and thy years shall have no end.

**Context:** Psalm 102 — The psalmist contrasts the impermanence of the created order with God's enduring nature. The heavens and earth will perish and be changed like a garment, but God remains the same. Quoted in Heb 1:10-12 and applied to Christ.

**Notes:** v.26 — 'They shall perish' (hemah yo'vedu) + 'wax old like a garment' (kabbeghed yivlu) — the heavens and earth are not permanent. 'As a vesture shalt thou change them' (tachaliphem veyachalopu) — the verb chalaph means to change/exchange, suggesting transformation rather than annihilation. Paired with Isa 51:6 for OT cosmic dissolution background.

**Context:** Isaiah 13 — Oracle against Babylon. Isaiah compares Babylon's coming destruction to God's overthrow of Sodom and Gomorrah. The Sodom paradigm is applied to a future judgment on a world power.

**Notes:** Uses mahpekath (H4114, overthrow) — the same root as the 'overthrow' of Sodom in Gen 19:25,29. The Sodom paradigm becomes a template for prophetic judgment oracles throughout the OT (cf. Jer 49:18, Amo 4:11, Deut 29:23).

### Earth utterly broken, dissolved, moved exceedingly — cosmic dissolution in Isaiah's apocalypse

> 19 The earth is utterly broken down, the earth is clean dissolved, the earth is moved exceedingly. 20 The earth shall reel to and fro like a drunkard, and shall be removed like a cottage; and the transgression thereof shall be heavy upon it; and it shall fall, and not rise again.

**Context:** Isaiah 24 — The 'Isaiah apocalypse' (chs. 24-27). Universal judgment on the earth. The threefold 'the earth is...' emphasizes totality. The language of dissolution and removal provides OT prophetic background for Peter's cosmic dissolution in 2 Pet 3:10-12.

**Notes:** v.19 — ro'ah hitro'a'ah ha'arets, por hitporera'ah erets, mot hitmotetah arets — three intensified verbal phrases (infinitive absolute + finite verb) describing the earth's complete disintegration. v.20 — 'shall not rise again' (lo' tosiyph qum) indicates irreversible judgment. The sin-judgment causation is explicit: 'the transgression thereof shall be heavy upon it.'

### God swallows up death forever — eschatological promise paralleling Rev 21:4

> He will swallow up death in victory; and the Lord GOD will wipe away tears from off all faces; and the rebuke of his people shall he take away from off all the earth: for the LORD hath spoken [it].

**Context:** Isaiah 25 — Part of the Isaiah apocalypse (chs. 24-27). After the cosmic judgment of ch. 24, God swallows up death and wipes away tears — the same vision fulfilled in Rev 21:4. The pattern: judgment followed by new-creation restoration.

**Notes:** Quoted/echoed in 1 Cor 15:54 ('death is swallowed up in victory') and Rev 21:4 ('God shall wipe away all tears... no more death'). The creation-judgment-restoration pattern mirrors the flood sequence: corruption → flood → new world. Here: sin/death → cosmic judgment (ch. 24) → death swallowed up.

**Context:** Amos 4 — God recounts past judgments on Israel as warnings. The Sodom overthrow is used as a comparison for divine judgment already experienced. 'A firebrand plucked out of the burning' — partial deliverance from fire judgment.

'Firebrand plucked out of the burning' (ud mutzal misrepha) — even in fire judgment, God rescues a remnant.

### Noah's altar sacrifice and God's promise — post-flood covenant foundation

> 20 And Noah builded an altar unto the LORD; and took of every clean beast, and of every clean fowl, and offered burnt offerings on the altar. 21 And the LORD smelled a sweet savour; and the LORD said in his heart, I will not again curse the ground any more for man's sake; for the imagination of man's heart [is] evil from his youth; neither will I again smite any more every thing living, as I have done. 22 While the earth remaineth, seedtime and harvest, and cold and heat, and summer and winter, and day and night shall not cease.

**Context:** Genesis 8 — After the flood, Noah offers sacrifice and God responds with a promise. God acknowledges that human nature has not changed ('the imagination of man's heart is evil from his youth') but promises not to destroy again 'as I have done' — by flood. The promise is for the duration of the earth's existence ('while the earth remaineth').

**Notes:** v.21 — 'I will not again curse the ground' (lo'-osif leqallel et-ha'adamah) and 'neither will I again smite any more every thing living, as I have done' (velo'-osif od lehakkot et-kol-chay ka'asher asitiy). The qualifier 'as I have done' (ka'asher asitiy) limits the promise to the flood mode of destruction, paralleling Gen 9:11's specificity about mabbul. v.22 — 'while the earth remaineth' (od kol-yeme ha'arets) implies the earth has a terminus.

The cosmic dissolution imagery — heaven departing, mountains and islands moved — provides a Revelation parallel to Peter's fire-dissolution language. ### Punishment of Jerusalem greater than overthrow of Sodom — Sodom as judgment benchmark

> For the punishment of the iniquity of the daughter of my people is greater than the punishment of the sin of Sodom, that was overthrown as in a moment, and no hands stayed on her.

**Context:** Lamentations 4 — Jeremiah compares Jerusalem's suffering to Sodom's overthrow. Sodom's destruction was swift ('as in a moment'), while Jerusalem's suffering is prolonged and therefore worse. Sodom as the baseline for divine judgment.

### Land becomes brimstone, salt, burning 'like the overthrow of Sodom' — covenant curse

### Sodom's sin identified — pride, fullness of bread, idleness, neglect of poor, abomination

> 49 Behold, this was the iniquity of thy sister Sodom, pride, fulness of bread, and abundance of idleness was in her and in her daughters, neither did she strengthen the hand of the poor and needy. 50 And they were haughty, and committed abomination before me: therefore I took them away as I saw [good].

**Context:** Ezekiel 16 — God compares Jerusalem to Sodom. Ezekiel identifies Sodom's specific sins: pride, abundance, idleness, neglect of the poor, haughtiness, and abomination. The moral corruption that warranted fire judgment parallels the moral corruption that warranted the flood (Gen 6:5-7,11-12).

**Notes:** The moral profile of Sodom — pride, abundance without generosity, idleness, abomination — mirrors the pre-flood corruption described in Gen 6:5,11-12 ('every imagination... only evil continually,' 'earth filled with violence'). Both the flood and Sodom's fire were responses to comprehensive moral corruption, reinforcing the typological parallel.

### My Spirit shall not always strive with man — divine patience has limits before judgment

> And the LORD said, My spirit shall not always strive with man, for that he also [is] flesh: yet his days shall be an hundred and twenty years.

**Context:** Genesis 6 — God sets a 120-year limit before the flood judgment. Divine patience has a terminus. This pattern — extended patience followed by decisive judgment — recurs in Peter's argument (2 Pet 3:9, 'longsuffering').

**Notes:** ruachi (my spirit/Spirit) + lo'-yadon (shall not strive/contend) + le'olam (forever/always). The 120 years represents a grace period before judgment. Peter picks up this longsuffering theme in 2 Pet 3:9 ('not willing that any should perish') and 3:15 ('the longsuffering of our Lord is salvation'). The pattern: divine patience → warning → limit reached → judgment — applies to both flood and fire.

### The LORD shut him in — God closes door of ark before flood judgment

> And they that went in, went in male and female of all flesh, as God had commanded him: and the LORD shut him in.

**Context:** Genesis 7 — God shuts Noah into the ark before the flood. The closing of the ark door by God himself marks the moment when salvation opportunity ends and judgment is irrevocable. A potential type of the close of probation before final fire judgment.

**Notes:** vayyisgor YHWH ba'ado ('and the LORD shut him in') — God himself seals the ark. This is a divine act of both protection (sealing Noah in safety) and judgment (sealing the wicked out). The 'shutting in' before the flood may parallel the concept of probationary close before fire judgment — once the door is shut, the fate of all is sealed.

**Context:** Matthew 13 — Parables of the tares (v.42) and the dragnet (v.50). Both parables end with the wicked cast into a 'furnace of fire' (kaminon tou pyros). Fire as the instrument of final separation and judgment.

**Context:** Revelation 21 — Immediately after the new-creation promise (Rev 21:1-7), John describes the fate of the wicked: the lake of fire and brimstone, which is the second death. The fire+brimstone formula connects directly to the Sodom paradigm (Gen 19:24).

### LORD comes with fire and chariots like whirlwind — fire judgment and universal judgment on all flesh

> 15 For, behold, the LORD will come with fire, and with his chariots like a whirlwind, to render his anger with fury, and his rebuke with flames of fire. 16 For by fire and by his sword will the LORD plead with all flesh: and the slain of the LORD shall be many.

**Context:** Isaiah 66 — The final chapter of Isaiah. The LORD comes 'with fire' to render judgment on 'all flesh.' This appears in the same chapter as the second 'new heavens and new earth' reference (Isa 66:22), connecting fire judgment directly to new creation within the OT prophetic framework.

**Notes:** v.15 — ba'esh (H784, 'with fire') + lahavot esh ('flames of fire'). v.16 — ba'esh (with fire) + nishpat et-kol-basar ('plead with all flesh') — universal scope. The same chapter contains both the fire judgment (vv.15-16) and the new heavens/earth promise (v.22) and the unquenchable fire reference (v.24). Isaiah 66 thus contains the complete judgment-to-new-creation sequence within a single prophetic chapter.
