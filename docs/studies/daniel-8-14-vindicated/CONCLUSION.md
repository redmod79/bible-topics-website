# Conclusion: Daniel 8:14 — 'Vindicated' as the Better Rendering, the Fitting Response to 8:13, and the Larger Category That Includes Cleansing

## Question

In Daniel 8:14, is the Hebrew word translated 'cleansed' (KJV) more appropriately rendered 'vindicated'? Is 'vindicated' the proper response to the question posed in Daniel 8:13? And does the meaning 'vindicated' include cleansing?

## Summary Answer

Yes to all three, unified as one coherent hierarchy — confident on the direction, calibrated on two live residuals. The five identity axes each carry their own confidence, and the openness on the material axis does not weaken the functional conclusion.

- **Grammatical object (explicit):** *nitsdaq* is predicated of *qodesh* (the sanctuary) as its subject in a divine passive. The verb is predicated of the sanctuary (qodesh) as its subject in a divine passive: God sets right the very sanctuary that verse 13 showed being trampled.
- **Functional-conceptual identity (necessary inference):** the word names a forensic verdict, not a ritual purification. The word names a forensic verdict of vindication — the larger category that includes ritual cleansing while reaching beyond it, not a denial of it.
- **Material subtype (uncertain, and it stays uncertain):** As the sole OT Niphal of the verb, its force stays open between 'declared righteous' and 'restored to rightful state' — both justice-field, neither 'cleansed'. This openness is a calibration on the precise nuance only; it does not reopen the cleansing reading and does not weaken the functional verdict.
- **Violated norm / response to 8:13 (necessary inference):** Daniel 8:13 poses an injustice problem — transgression, desolation, trampling, no purity word in view — and an injustice problem takes a justice answer.
- **Redemptive action (reasonable inference):** The verdict is the declared result of a completed atoning and cleansing process, not a rival to it — inclusion rather than displacement.

## Key Verses

> **The kaphar+tsedeq bridge (Daniel 9:24)** Seventy weeks are determined upon thy people and upon thy holy city, to finish the transgression, and to make an end of sins, and to make reconciliation for iniquity, and to bring in everlasting righteousness, and to seal up the vision and prophecy, and to anoint the most Holy.

> **The Servant's courtroom vindication (Isaiah 50:8)** He is near that justifieth me; who will contend with me? let us stand together: who is mine adversary? let him come near to me.

> **tsadaq beside a purity verb (Job 25:4)** How then can man be justified with God? or how can he be clean that is born of a woman?

## Analysis

### Step 1: What the crux clause says

Daniel 8:14 closes the little-horn vision with a single answer clause. The parser fixes the facts. The verb behind the KJV "cleansed" is *nitsdaq*, a Niphal Perfect 3ms of *tsadaq* (H6663), preceded by a conjunctive waw, with *qodesh* (the sanctuary/holiness) as its grammatical subject. This is the sole Niphal of *tsadaq* in the whole Hebrew Old Testament — a one-of-a-kind stem-form. The root is the justice/righteousness root, not the ritual-cleansing root *taher* (H2891). The Niphal carries passive force and names no agent, which makes it a divine passive.

Reading the grammar is not inference. The verb is predicated of the sanctuary (qodesh) as its subject in a divine passive: God sets right the very sanctuary that verse 13 showed being trampled. This settles the grammatical-object axis at explicit strength. The sanctuary receives the action; God is the unnamed one who acts. That fact grounds every downstream question, because it tells us the word in dispute is a passive of the justice root applied to the sanctuary.

One textual complication belongs at the outset and is engaged honestly later: the local Greek of this verse reads "καὶ καθαρισθήσεται τὸ ἅγιον" — the verb *katharisthesetai*, from *katharizo* (G2511), a cleanse-verb, not the justify-verb *dikaioo* (G1344). So the dispute is real at both the Hebrew-root and the Greek-versional levels.

### Step 2: The lexical range of tsadaq — is 'vindicated' the more accurate rendering?

The word study fixes *tsadaq* as a forensic/relational verb, not a purity verb. Across 41 OT occurrences (54 KJV entries), its stems are Qal 22, Hiphil 12, Piel 5, Niphal 1 (Dan 8:14), Hithpael 1. The KJV renders it "just/justify/righteous" in every occurrence except Daniel 8:14, where alone it reads "cleansed." The primary LXX renderings are *dikaioo* (21x) and *dikaios* (22x), with *krino* (judge) 8x, and never *katharizo* across the corpus. It collocates with courtroom vocabulary — *mishpat*, *shaphat*, *tsaddiq*, *rasha*. The cognate family confirms the field: *tsedeq* (H6664, 119x), *tsedaqah* (H6666, 165x), and *tsaddiq* (H6662, 210x) all live in the righteousness/justice register.

Honesty requires one qualification, which the study makes rather than hides. Strong's own gloss list for H6663 does include "cleanse, clear," so the lexicon is not ignorant of a cleansing sense. But that is a secondary/derived sense. Tested against actual usage, 53 of 54 KJV renderings are justice-words, and the "clear" element of the gloss ("clear oneself," as in the Hithpael) is itself the forensic "acquit/declare-innocent" sense, not a purity wash. The dispute is therefore about primary versus secondary sense, and about a "cleansed" rendering that is unique to this one verse.

The forensic base sense is not asserted from the lexicon alone; it is fixed from the courtroom corpus. Deuteronomy 25:1 sets *tsadaq* as a judge's declarative verdict, the exact antonym of "condemn." Proverbs 17:15 pairs "justifieth the wicked" with "condemneth the just" as the two opposite verdicts. Job 40:8 frames being righteous as the outcome of winning a legal case inside a *mishpat* frame. Isaiah 50:8 saturates the verb with litigation vocabulary — *riv* (contend at law), *amad* (stand as litigants), *baal mishpat* (adversary at law). Isaiah 43:9, 43:26, and 45:25 are trial scenes, individual and corporate, all rendered *dikaioo*. Psalm 143:2 places the verb inside an explicit judgment frame before the divine Judge. Every one of these is a courtroom verdict with zero purity content.

Weighing the morphology, the forensic word-family, and the Strong's-gloss objection together, "vindicated" (or "set right") is the more accurate rendering than "cleansed." A passive of a justice verb yields "be declared/set right," not "be purified." "Cleansed" under-translates by dropping the verdict content. This is a necessary inference on the Hebrew — denying it requires contradicting both the parser and the corpus. The one residual is the hapax stem, which is a calibration on precision, not a defeater. As the sole OT Niphal of the verb, its force stays open between 'declared righteous' and 'restored to rightful state' — both justice-field, neither 'cleansed'. Whether the passive force is declared-righteous or restored-to-rightful-state is not decidable from the sole OT Niphal and is left genuinely open. Because both options sit in the justice field, this openness never rescues "cleansed."

### Step 3: The authorial vocabulary audit — Daniel chose tsadaq deliberately

Daniel possessed the cleansing and atonement vocabulary and used it elsewhere. Leviticus 16:19 and 16:30 use *taher* (H2891) for cleansing the altar and the people on the Day of Atonement — the exact sanctuary context Daniel 8:14 evokes, with the *kaphar*-to-*taher* sequence in one verse (Lev 16:30). *taher* is the standard cleansing verb (115 KJV instances; LXX *katharizo* 61x). One chapter after 8:14, Daniel 9:24 uses *lekapper* (*kaphar*, H3722, "to make reconciliation for iniquity") — proving the atonement verb was in his active lexicon. Daniel 12:10 uses a third register again — *barar*, *laban*, *tsaraph* — for the end-time purifying of the righteous. So Daniel commands three distinct registers and selects each for its context. His choice of the justice verb *tsadaq* in 8:14 is deliberate, not a lexical gap.

The audit guards against overreach. *taher* is not a narrow word. Malachi 3:3 uses it for a deep, refining, eschatological purification of the Levites that issues in "an offering in righteousness," and Ezekiel 36:25 uses it for a covenant-wide moral cleansing. So the inclusion case cannot rest on shrinking *taher*; it must rest on *tsadaq*'s own breadth.

The deliberate choice means one of three things: "more than cleansing" (inclusion), "not cleansing at all" (displacement), or "cleansing primary." The audit converges on inclusion. The base sense Daniel selected is forensic (the courtroom corpus). But the same root's field overlaps the purity field (the parallelism data below). And Daniel 9:24 sets *kaphar* directly beside *tsedeq* in one breath — atonement issuing in righteousness — showing the registers are joined, not opposed. The verdict pronounces the cleansing it presupposes complete. The verdict is the declared result of a completed atoning and cleansing process, not a rival to it — inclusion rather than displacement.

### Step 4: The parallelism evidence — does vindication include cleansing?

Tested at its strongest point, the inclusion thesis holds at calibrated, modest weight. *tsadaq* stands in synonymous poetic parallel with purity words in three couplets. Job 25:4 pairs *tsadaq* ("be justified") with *zakah* (H2135, "be clean/pure"). Job 4:17 pairs *tsadaq* ("be just") with *taher* ("be pure"), and the local LXX renders that couplet with *katharos*, the same kathar- word-group as Daniel 8:14's *katharisthesetai* — Davidson's claim verified against the corpus. Psalm 51:4 pairs *tsadaq* with *zakah* inside a psalm whose cleansing petition (v.7) uses *taher* and *kabas*, so verdict and cleansing co-occur in one setting. *zakah* and *bar* (H1249) are genuine purity terms, so the overlap is real.

The negative control keeps the conclusion honest. Job 9:2 uses *tsadaq* with no purity term ("how should man be just with God?"), showing the base sense is forensic and the cleansing overlap is contextually activated only where a purity word is paired. So cleansing is an included facet in cultic/relational contexts, not the bare lexical core. Two limits apply: the paralleled purity corpus is small (*zakah* 11x, *bar* 9x), and the couplets come from wisdom poetry, a genre distinct from Daniel's apocalyptic vision. So the field-overlap transfers to Daniel 8:14 as a supported possibility grounded in the root's breadth, not as a dominant pattern.

Job 4:17's LXX rendering is precise: the Greek renders the second (*taher*) colon with *katharos*, not *tsadaq* itself. The datum shows the couplet is treated as a purity-parallel — the fields overlap — not that *tsadaq* lexically means "cleanse." That is exactly what inclusion predicts and what displacement and cleansing-primary do not.

### Step 5: The Zechariah 3 model and the DOA-as-judgment

Zechariah 3:1-5 is the clearest OT narrative model of cleansing within vindication. A heavenly court convenes: Satan the accuser is rebuked, Joshua the high priest stands accused in filthy garments, and the verdict is spoken in one utterance. That single verdict removes defilement ("take away the filthy garments," "I have caused thine iniquity to pass from thee") and confers positive standing ("I will clothe thee with change of raiment," and a fair mitre "set," *sum* H7760, on the head). These are two movements of one judicial act, not competing descriptions. The scene shares the accuser-court-verdict shape of Daniel 7 and Job. Its honest limit is that Zechariah 3 uses neither *tsadaq* nor *taher*, so it is a structural model of the inclusion hierarchy, not a lexical proof; the lexical inclusion claim rests on the parallelism data.

The Day of Atonement itself was both cleansing and judgment. Leviticus 16:30 gives the cleansing side (*kaphar* issuing in *taher*). Leviticus 23:29-30 gives the judgment side: those not afflicted are "cut off," those who work are "destroyed" from among the people — judicial separations decided on the day. Because the day unites cleansing and judgment, a judgment-word (*tsadaq*) is a fitting antitype-word that includes rather than displaces the cleansing.

### Step 6: The forensic frame of Daniel 7 and the judgment as vindication

Daniel 7 supplies the courtroom that Daniel 8:14 compresses into one word. The Ancient of Days is seated, thousands minister, "the judgment (*din*, H1780) was set, and the books were opened" (7:9-10). The verdict is two-sided: "judgment was given to the saints" (7:22) and "the judgment shall sit, and they shall take away his dominion" (7:26). *din* (H1780) is the Aramaic judgment-word occurring across these court scenes. The structure runs from the horn's attack (7:8) to the seated court (7:9-10) to the verdict for the saints and against the horn (7:22, 7:26).

Daniel 7 and Daniel 8:14 answer the same horn's assault. Daniel 7 renders the answer as a full court deciding for the wronged; Daniel 8:14 renders it as a single divine-passive justice-verb, with the same *qodesh* trampled in the question set right in the answer. The same referent, the same act-type (a divine forensic setting-right), and the same beneficiary bind the two: the single *nitsdaq* names the judgment Daniel 7's court renders in expanded form. This fixes the setting-right as a judicial verdict, not a ritual purification.

### Step 7: The response to Daniel 8:13 — an injustice problem takes a justice answer

Daniel 8:13 names its problem in explicit vocabulary. The parser confirms four operative terms: *pesha* (H6588, transgression), *shamem* (H8074, be desolate), *natan/tet* (H5414, give-over), and *mirmac* (H4823, trodden under foot). *mirmac*, from *ramas* (to trample), means "abasement," dominant KJV "trodden down." No purity or defilement verb (*taher*, *tame*) appears anywhere in the verse. Daniel 8:11-12 frame the same problem as usurpation and assault — the daily taken away, the sanctuary cast down, the host given over "by reason of transgression." Daniel 8:13 poses an injustice problem — transgression, desolation, trampling, no purity word in view — and an injustice problem takes a justice answer.

The syntax binds the answer to the question. The "unto" (*ad*) of v.14 answers the "how long" (*ad-matay*) of v.13 as a lock-and-key. And *qodesh*, the object of trampling in v.13, becomes the subject of vindication in v.14 — the object-to-subject inversion. So the very entity assaulted in the question is the entity set right in the answer. A justice-word (*nitsdaq*, be set right/vindicated) fits the wrong 8:13 names; a purity-answer ("cleansed") would answer a defilement problem the text does not primarily pose. The absence of *taher*/*tame* across Daniel 8 is a selective, in-domain but thin silence that corroborates this reading without carrying it — the verdict rests on the present vocabulary and syntax, not on the silence.

This response verdict is established on a premise chain independent of the lexical verdict. The two share only the bare morphological fact that the verb is *tsadaq*. Neither smuggled in the other. They converge on the same answer-word for the same verse from different evidence — an injustice question takes a justice answer, and the justice-word is the larger category that includes the cleansing it exceeds.

### Step 8: The redemptive-action axis — process versus verdict

Daniel 9:24 is the internal-to-Daniel bridge, the only OT verse pairing *kaphar* (atone) with *tsedeq* (everlasting righteousness), with the atonement clauses issuing in the righteousness clause. Isaiah 53:11 grounds the justifying verdict ("justify many") in the Servant's sin-bearing ("for he shall bear their iniquities") — the verdict is the declared result of a completed atonement. Romans 3:24-26 realizes the same shape in the NT: the propitiation (*hilasterion*, v.25) grounds God being "just and the justifier" (*dikaios* + *dikaioo*, v.26). Hebrews 9:22-23 supplies the cleansing process — the heavenly things "purified" with better sacrifices — whose completion the Daniel 8:14 verdict pronounces.

The redemptive-action axis therefore reads *nitsdaq* as the declared verdict of a completed atonement/cleansing process, inclusion not displacement. The verdict is the declared result of a completed atoning and cleansing process, not a rival to it — inclusion rather than displacement. This axis is graded reasonable inference, because Hebrews does not itself state the process/verdict distinction; it is read across passages. One honest limit: the NT bridge runs through the Hebrew *tsadaq* and its normal LXX rendering, since Daniel 8:14's own local LXX reads *katharizo*, not *dikaioo* — so this is not a direct Greek-to-Greek link at the crux verse.

### Step 9: The widest referent — cosmic vindication

The *tsadaq*/*dikaios* field reaches referents a cleansing word cannot carry. Revelation 15:3-4 predicates *dikaios* ("just") of God's ways; Revelation 16:5-7 declares God and His judgments "righteous"; Revelation 19:2 declares "true and righteous are his judgments" and ties it to God "avenging the blood of his servants" — the same injustice-to-justice shape as Daniel 7:22 and 8:14 at cosmic scale. One does not "cleanse" God's ways or "purify" a verdict of vengeance; only the righteousness field carries these. Since *tsadaq* is rendered by the same dik- word-group, this shows the righteousness field is the larger category that exceeds "cleansed." This reinforces the exceeds-cleansing half of the hierarchy without denying the cleansing it includes.

### Step 10: The three sub-answers as one hierarchy

The three sub-answers are not in tension. An injustice question (8:13) takes a justice answer (8:14 *nitsdaq*, vindication), and that justice-word is the larger category that includes the cleansing "cleansed" names but exceeds it. "Vindicated is better" and "includes cleansing" are two faces of one claim, not rivals. The identity verdict (better rendering plus includes cleansing) and the response verdict (fits 8:13's injustice) rest on disjoint premise chains and were established independently, so their agreement is genuine, not circular. The answer is confident on direction and calibrated on two residuals it refuses to collapse: the hapax underdetermination and the local-Greek cleanse-verb.

## Word Studies

*tsadaq* (H6663), the disputed verb, is forensic at its core: 41 OT occurrences, KJV "just/justify/righteous" everywhere except Daniel 8:14, LXX *dikaioo*/*dikaios* and never *katharizo*, courtroom collocates. Strong's includes "cleanse, clear" as a secondary sense, whose "clear" component is the forensic "acquit" sense. The cognates *tsedeq* (H6664, 119x), *tsedaqah* (H6666, 165x), and *tsaddiq* (H6662, 210x) confirm the righteousness field.

The contrast verbs Daniel bypassed: *taher* (H2891, 115 KJV instances; LXX *katharizo* 61x), the standard cleansing verb of Leviticus 16; *kaphar* (H3722, 130 occurrences), the blood-atonement verb Daniel used in 9:24. The purity words that parallel *tsadaq*: *zakah* (H2135, 11x, "be clean/pure") and *bar* (H1249, 9x, "pure/clean").

The Daniel 8:13 injustice vocabulary: *pesha* (H6588, 95x, "transgression/revolt"), *shamem* (H8074, 103x, "be desolate"), *natan* (H5414, "give/give-over"), and *mirmac* (H4823, 8x, "trodden down"). The judgment word of Daniel 7: *din* (H1780, Aramaic, 5x, all in Daniel's court scenes).

On the Greek side: *dikaioo* (G1344, NT 48x; the LXX rendering of *tsadaq*) — the local LXX audit covers 29 tokens in 28 verses, all uniformly forensic, never cleansing. *katharizo* (G2511, NT 37x; LXX rendering of *taher* 61x) is the cleanse-verb at Daniel 8:14's local LXX and at Hebrews 9:23. *dikaios* (G1342, NT 81x) is the adjective predicated of God's ways and judgments in Revelation.

## Difficult Passages

**The local Greek of Daniel 8:14 reads a cleanse-verb.** The prior study daniel-8-14-cleansed-hebrew-meaning had established that *tsadaq* is rendered *dikaioo*/*dikaios*/*krino* in the LXX and never *katharizo*, and traced Daniel 8:14's cleanse-rendering to the Vulgate ("mundabitur") and to a Theodotion Greek "katharisthesetai" reading Daniel 8:14 through the DOA lens of Leviticus 16. The present study narrows that finding. The prior research assumed an Old Greek "justify" versus Theodotion "cleanse" split at Daniel 8:14. The mandated verification corrects this. The local LXX reads "καὶ καθαρισθήσεται τὸ ἅγιον" — *katharisthesetai* (from *katharizo*, G2511), not *dikaioo*. The corpus surfaces only this one reading and does not distinguish Old Greek from Theodotion, so the assumed split cannot be sustained from the local evidence. This is genuine support for the cleansing tradition; an early Greek reader rendered *nitsdaq* with a purity verb, and Job 4:17 shows the LXX will render a *tsadaq*-line with the *katharos* word-group where a purity term is paralleled. Two things keep the Hebrew-side verdict intact. Across all 29 local *dikaioo* occurrences, the LXX renders *tsadaq* forensically and never cleanses, and Isaiah 45:25's *dikaiothesontai* is a direct in-corpus comparandum — so Daniel 8:14's *katharizo* is an outlier, and the KJV "cleansed" descends from the Greek/Latin cleanse-tradition (to the Vulgate *mundabitur*), not from the Hebrew verb's core sense. And because the corpus cannot sort an early interpretive reading from a Theodotion-type overlay, cleansing-primary and displacement both stay live rather than being pre-decided. The witness lowers confidence that cleansing is excluded while leaving "vindicated" the more accurate rendering of the source term.

**Strong's lists "cleanse, clear" for H6663.** The study grants the lexicon lists a cleansing sense and does not claim *tsadaq* never means cleanse. But 53 of 54 KJV renderings are justice-words, the LXX never renders *tsadaq* with *katharizo* elsewhere, and the "clear" element is the forensic acquit sense. So the gloss marks a secondary sense, and the unique "cleansed" rendering at Daniel 8:14 is plausibly downstream of the Greek/Latin tradition. This keeps inclusion open (cleansing as an included secondary facet) rather than a hard displacement claim.

**Hebrews 9:23 applies a cleanse-verb to the heavenly sanctuary.** The prior study sv-19-righteousness-defilement-vindication settles this by inclusion, and this study carries that resolution. Hebrews 9:22-23 describes the process — a purification accomplished by better blood, "without shedding of blood is no remission." Daniel 8:14's *nitsdaq* names the verdict — the declared outcome. These answer different questions. The broader category (*tsadaq*/vindication) includes the narrower one (*taher*/*katharizo*): you cannot vindicate what is still contaminated, but vindication exceeds mere purification. So Hebrews 9:23 confirms a genuine cleansing belongs to the heavenly reality — defeating any strong-displacement claim that cleansing is excluded — while leaving "vindicated" correct for Daniel's Hebrew verb. On its own, it removes strong displacement but does not rank cleansing-primary against inclusion, since both predict a heavenly cleansing.

**The hapax stem leaves the precise nuance open.** Daniel 8:14 is the sole Niphal of *tsadaq*, so the grammar cannot decide between "be vindicated/declared righteous" and "be put right/restored to rightful state." This is a genuine underdetermination, carried as a calibration. Both candidate nuances are justice-field readings, and neither is "cleansed," so the openness does not reopen the cleansing reading or weaken the functional verdict.

**The parallelism corpus is small and cross-genre.** The inclusion case rests on a small purity corpus (*zakah* 11x, *bar* 9x) drawn from wisdom poetry, a genre distinct from Daniel's apocalyptic vision. So the overlap is real but modest, and inclusion is held with calibrated, defeatable confidence — grounded in the root's breadth, weighed alongside the pure-forensic controls, not on shrinking *taher*.

## Candidate Dispositions

**Rank 1 — Vindication includes cleansing (supported, calibrated).** Vindication that includes cleansing is the best-supported reading: 'vindicated' is the larger forensic category that contains the cleansing it declares complete, held with calibrated confidence because the verb is a one-of-a-kind stem and the supporting parallels carry modest weight. It is the only reading consistent with all the evidence: the forensic base sense, the deliberate authorial choice of the justice-root, the field-overlap with purity verbs (Job 25:4, Job 4:17), the Zechariah 3 one-verdict model, the Daniel 9:24 *kaphar*+*tsedeq* bridge, the Romans 3 atonement-grounds-verdict frame, and the Hebrews 9:23 heavenly cleansing whose completion the verdict pronounces.

**Rank 2 — Vindication displaces cleansing (strong form defeated, weak form survives).** The strong displacement claim — that cleansing is excluded from the word's meaning — is defeated, because cleansing is positively attested in the surrounding evidence; what survives is the narrower, correct point that 'vindicated' is the more precise rendering and 'cleansed' by itself under-translates. Daniel 9:24 joins *kaphar* with *tsedeq*, Zechariah 3:4 holds cleansing and conferred standing in one verdict, Job 25:4 sets *tsadaq* in synonymous parallel with the purity verb *zakah*, and the local LXX *katharizo* plus Strong's "cleanse, clear" gloss attest a genuine cleansing note — so "cleansing not in view" is unsustainable. The point of agreement with the Ford critique is that *tsadaq* is the more precise word and "cleansed" alone under-translates by dropping the verdict content.

**Rank 3 — Cleansing primary (defeated on the Hebrew, survives as a versional minority).** Ritual cleansing as the primary sense is defeated on the Hebrew, where the verb's base meaning is a courtroom verdict; it survives only as a disfavored minority reading carried by the Greek witness of the verse, which the local corpus cannot sort from a later overlay. The base sense of *tsadaq* is a courtroom verdict (Deut 25:1, Isa 50:8, Job 9:2 with no purity term), and the LXX renders *tsadaq* with *dikaioo*/*dikaios* and never *katharizo* elsewhere, so "cleanse" is a contextually activated facet, not the base meaning. It is not driven to zero — the local *katharizo* and the hapax Niphal keep it alive — but it is the least-supported candidate.

## Conclusion

Daniel 8:14 answers all three of its questions as one hierarchy. "Vindicated" is the more accurate rendering than "cleansed" (Dan 8:14; Deut 25:1; Isa 50:8; Job 9:2): the verb is the forensic root *tsadaq* in a Niphal divine passive predicated of *qodesh*, rendered "just/justify/righteous" everywhere else and *dikaioo* in the LXX, so "cleansed" under-translates by dropping the verdict content. This holds as a necessary inference on the Hebrew, calibrated by the hapax stem and the local-Greek witness.

"Vindicated" is the fitting response to Daniel 8:13 (Dan 8:11-12; 8:13; 8:14): the verse poses an injustice problem — *pesha*, *shamem*, *natan*, *mirmac*, with no purity verb — and 8:14 answers with a single justice-word, the *ad*/*ad-matay* lock-and-key and the *qodesh* object-to-subject inversion binding answer to question. Daniel 7's seated court (Dan 7:9-10, 22, 26) renders the same verdict in expanded form.

And "vindicated" includes cleansing, as inclusion not displacement (Dan 9:24; Job 25:4; Job 4:17; Zech 3:4; Rom 3:24-26; Heb 9:22-23): the verdict is the larger forensic category that subsumes the atonement/cleansing process it declares complete. Strong displacement is defeated because cleansing is positively attested in the surrounding evidence; cleansing-primary is defeated on the Hebrew, surviving only as a disfavored versional-minority reading. The five identity axes stand at their own confidences — grammatical object explicit, functional identity by necessary inference, violated norm by necessary inference, redemptive action by reasonable inference, and the material subtype genuinely uncertain — and the material openness does not weaken the functional conclusion, because both open nuances remain justice-field readings and neither is "cleansed." The whole resolves into one coherent claim: an injustice question takes a justice answer, and the justice-word is the larger category that includes the cleansing it exceeds.
